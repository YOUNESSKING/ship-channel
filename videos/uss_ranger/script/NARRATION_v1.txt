In December, twenty fourteen, the United States Navy let go of an aircraft carrier for one cent. The headlines said she was sold for a penny.

She had flown combat missions over Vietnam and in the Gulf War. She had been on the big screen, in Top Gun, and in Star Trek.

A single penny, for a warship more than a thousand feet long, that had served for nearly thirty-six years.

This is the USS Ranger.

And the strangest part of the deal was not the price. It was who paid whom.

So how does a ship like this end up worth less than nothing? And what is left of her today?

Start with the place. Bremerton, Washington, on Puget Sound, across the water from Seattle.

Next to the naval shipyard lies a quiet row of warships nobody uses any more. The Navy calls them inactive ships. Sailors call it the mothball fleet.

Old warships wait there, rusting slowly, while the Navy decides what to do with them.

For twenty years, the Ranger sat there. Grey, silent, and tied to a pier. Her catapults were cold. Her hangar deck was empty. No jet had landed on her since nineteen ninety-three.

On the twenty-second of December, twenty fourteen, the Navy announced her future. A contract, to a company called International Shipbreaking, in Brownsville, Texas.

The job was to tow her away, and take her apart. The price was one cent.

The story spread fast. Navy carrier sold for a penny. Top Gun ship goes for one cent.

It sounded like a joke. A carrier that cost the country a fortune, given away for the smallest coin it makes.

But nearly every part of that headline hides a surprise. To see what the Navy was letting go, go back to the summer of nineteen eighty-five.

San Diego Bay. A film crew from Paramount was making a movie about Navy fighter pilots. It was called Top Gun.

The Navy lent the production its ships and its jets. The scenes at sea were shot mostly on another carrier, the USS Enterprise.

But in the first weeks of filming, the crew also worked on the Ranger, while she was tied up across the bay, at Coronado.

So she was not the Top Gun carrier. She was one of them. And that was enough to follow her for the rest of her life.

There is an odd twist here. Her own crew had a nickname for her long before the movie. They called her the Top Gun of the Pacific. The name was already part of her story in nineteen eighty-two, at her twenty-fifth birthday. Four years before the film.

A year later, Hollywood came back. Star Trek Four needed an aircraft carrier called Enterprise.

The real Enterprise was not available. So the Ranger played her. The crew spent three days filming at North Island, in San Diego, for the scenes where two of the Starship's officers sneak aboard a Navy carrier.

Sailors in the background had caps with the Ranger's own name on them.

A few years after that, she had a small part in one more war film, Flight of the Intruder.

Today, the films are the reason most people know her name. But for the Navy, Hollywood was a few days in a career of nearly thirty-six years.

She was built at Newport News, Virginia. Her keel was laid in August, nineteen fifty-four. On the twenty-ninth of September, nineteen fifty-six, she was christened by the wife of Admiral Arthur Radford, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.

She was the third ship of the Forrestal class, the Navy's new supercarriers, built for the age of the jet. More than a thousand feet long. About fifty-six thousand tons empty, and over eighty thousand fully loaded.

She was commissioned in August, nineteen fifty-seven, at the Norfolk Naval Shipyard in Virginia. Her first captain was Charles Booth.

The next summer, she sailed from the Atlantic around Cape Horn, at the bottom of South America, to join the Pacific Fleet. Remember that voyage. She would make it once more.

From her home ports in California, she crossed to the Western Pacific more than twenty times.

In the summer of nineteen sixty-four, she sailed for the waters off Vietnam. Over the next nine years, she made seven cruises to the war, launching strikes from the Gulf of Tonkin.

It was on the Ranger that a new attack jet, the A-7 Corsair Two, went into combat in nineteen sixty-seven.

In nineteen eighty, during the Iran hostage crisis, she waited in the Arabian Sea.

In nineteen eighty-three, she was back there. On the first of November, a fire broke out in one of her main machinery rooms. It started at about ten to ten in the morning.

From the machinery room, it spread into the space next to it. Her crew fought it for about an hour. Then it flared up again, and burned until after midday.

Six sailors died, and thirty-five were hurt. Most of the injuries were from smoke, burns and heat.

The date was the first of November. Keep that date in mind.

In December, nineteen ninety, she left San Diego for the Persian Gulf. She reached her launch position on the fifteenth of January, nineteen ninety-one. Two days later, Desert Storm began.

Before dawn on the first day of the air war, her jets flew strikes against Iraq. On the third night, one of her A-6 bombers was lost. Over six weeks, her air wing flew thousands of missions. She came home to San Diego in June.

And in December, nineteen ninety-two, she was off Somalia, supporting the American landing there, Operation Restore Hope. Her planes flew reconnaissance, and stood ready to support the Marines going ashore.

That was her last cruise. On the tenth of July, nineteen ninety-three, the Ranger was decommissioned. Her crew walked off, and the flight deck went quiet.

She was towed north to Bremerton, and put into the reserve fleet.

For years, the Navy kept her in case it ever needed her again. It never did.

On the eighth of March, two thousand four, she was struck from the Naval Vessel Register. In plain words, she was no longer a Navy ship.

That opened a door. The Navy put her on its donation list. Any city or group that could take care of her could have her, as a museum.

There was a precedent. Carriers had been saved before. The Intrepid in New York. The Midway in San Diego. Each one a museum that millions of people have walked through.

One group tried to do the same for the Ranger. The USS Ranger Foundation, started in two thousand one by former crew and supporters.

Their plan was to bring her to Fairview, Oregon, on the Columbia River, near Portland. A floating museum, open to the public.

The Navy had rules. A new owner had to show it could pay for the first five years of running the ship.

The Foundation's own estimates ran from about ten million dollars to get her ready, up to thirty million in all.

By the Navy's count, the Foundation could show about one hundred and five thousand dollars, against a start-up bill of nearly thirty-two million. The Foundation said it had raised more than six hundred thousand. Either way, it was a long way short.

A Navy spokesman put it simply. No organization had been able to meet the Navy's minimum requirements.

Then there was the river. To reach Fairview, the Ranger would have had to pass a bridge across the Columbia. The Navy said the group never solved that problem either.

The Foundation kept trying for years. The money never came.

After eight years on the donation list, the Navy decided it could not keep waiting. In September, twenty twelve, it took her off the list. She would be scrapped.

Her supporters fought on. But in December, twenty fourteen, the contract came. The penny.

A month later, the Navy said it would not put her back on the donation list. It was over.

And here is the twist. The Ranger was never sold.

The Navy said it plainly. This is not a sales contract. It is a procurement contract.

In a sale, the buyer pays the Navy. Here, the Navy was the customer. It hired a shipbreaker to tow the carrier sixteen thousand miles, and cut her into pieces. And it paid for the job.

So why only one cent? Because of the steel.

The shipbreaker would keep everything it cut out of her, and sell it. Its price was the cost of the job, minus what it expected that metal to fetch.

For the Ranger, the scrap was worth about as much as the work. So the bid came down to almost nothing.

One cent was the lowest price the Navy could possibly have paid.

The Navy had set this up on purpose. It wrote one contract for all of its old conventional carriers, and let three shipbreakers compete for each ship. Each company looked at the hull, worked out what the tow and the cutting would cost, and what the metal would sell for. The lowest bid won.

All three of those yards were in the same place. Brownsville, Texas, at the southern tip of the state, a short sail from the Gulf of Mexico. For American carriers, it had become the end of the line.

And the Ranger was not the first. Her sister ship Forrestal had gone the same way, for one cent, in twenty thirteen. Then the Saratoga, in twenty fourteen. Also one cent.

But the deal is not always that good. Six months before the Ranger, the same company had taken the Constellation, a carrier of the next class. That time, the Navy paid three million dollars.

So the penny was not an insult. It was the best deal the Navy could get.

Years later, two more carriers went for the same price. The Kitty Hawk and the John F. Kennedy. One cent each.

On the fifth of March, twenty fifteen, at about nine in the morning, four harbour tugs eased the Ranger away from her pier in Bremerton. The Navy had expected the trip to take four to five months.

People lined the shore to watch. Some of them had served on board.

For the long haul, the job went to an ocean tug from Louisiana, the Crosby Leader.

She was too big for the Panama Canal. So there was only one way to Texas. All the way around South America.

Down the Pacific coast of two continents. Around the southern tip, the same waters she had rounded in nineteen fifty-eight, as a brand new ship.

Then north, up the Atlantic, and into the Gulf of Mexico.

About sixteen thousand miles. For four months, a ship that once launched jets moved at the speed of the tug in front of her.

On the twelfth of July, twenty fifteen, she reached the channel at Brownsville. It was a Sunday morning. Crowds stood on the jetties at South Padre Island to see her come in.

For the last few hours, she crept up the ship channel at about four knots, a walking pace, to the yard where she would end.

The whole trip had taken one hundred and twenty-nine days. A little over four months.

She was the second carrier to arrive that year. The Constellation was already there.

Then the cutting began.

Shipbreaking is slow work. First, workers strip out what can be reused or must be removed. Then the torches go to work, and the hull comes apart, section by section.

It took just over two years. More than fifty-six thousand tons of her were cut up and recycled. Most of the metal went to steel mills in the United States.

On the morning of the first of November, twenty seventeen, the last section of the Ranger came out.

The first of November. Thirty-four years to the day after the fire in her machinery room.

At nine fifty that morning, close to the minute the fire had started, the workers in Brownsville stopped for a moment of silence. For the six sailors who died on board.

So that is what happened to the carrier from the movies. The Navy paid a penny to make her disappear.

And the ship itself is gone.

But not all of her. More than five tons of her fittings went to the USS Lexington Museum, in Corpus Christi, Texas.

Gauges, windows and controls from the machinery room where the six men died went to her veterans, for a memorial.

Some of her brass and copper was turned into dog tags. And pieces of her flight deck were engraved and sold, to anyone who wanted to own a part of her.

Her sisters went the same way. Forrestal, Saratoga and Independence were all towed to Brownsville. Not one of the four became a museum.

Independence was the last. In twenty seventeen, she followed the Ranger's route, out of Bremerton and around South America, to the same yard. Her final voyage began while the Ranger was still being cut up.

So here is the question. Should the Navy have found a way to keep one of them? Or was one cent the right price for a carrier nobody could afford to keep?
