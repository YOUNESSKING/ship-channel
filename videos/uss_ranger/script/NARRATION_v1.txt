In December, twenty fourteen, the United States Navy let go of an aircraft carrier for one cent.

She had flown combat missions over Vietnam and in the Gulf War. She had been on the big screen, in Top Gun, and in Star Trek. The headlines said she was sold for one cent.

A single penny, for a warship that had served for nearly thirty-six years.

This is the USS Ranger.

And the strangest part of the deal was not the price. It was who paid whom.

So how does a ship like this end up worth less than nothing? And what is left of her today?

Start with the place. Bremerton, Washington, on Puget Sound. Behind the naval shipyard lies a quiet row of warships nobody uses any more.

For twenty years, the Ranger sat there. Grey, silent, and tied to a pier.

On the twenty-second of December, twenty fourteen, the Navy announced her future. A contract, to a company called International Shipbreaking, in Brownsville, Texas.

The job was to tow her away, and take her apart. The price was one cent.

The story spread fast. Navy carrier sold for a penny. Top Gun ship goes for one cent.

But nearly every part of that headline hides a surprise. To see what the Navy was throwing away, go back to the summer of nineteen eighty-five.

San Diego Bay. A film crew from Paramount was making a movie about Navy fighter pilots. It was called Top Gun.

The Navy lent the production its ships and its jets. Most of the carrier scenes were shot at sea on the USS Enterprise.

But in the first weeks of filming, the crew also worked on the Ranger, while she was tied up across the bay.

A year later, Hollywood came back. Star Trek Four needed an aircraft carrier called Enterprise.

The real Enterprise was not available. So the Ranger played her. Sailors in the background wore caps with the Ranger's own name on them.

Today, the films are the reason most people know her name. But for the Navy, Hollywood was a few days in a career of thirty-six years.

She was built at Newport News, Virginia. Her keel was laid in August, nineteen fifty-four. She was the third ship of the Forrestal class, the Navy's new supercarriers, built for the age of the jet.

She was commissioned in August, nineteen fifty-seven.

The next summer, she sailed from the Atlantic around Cape Horn, at the bottom of South America, to join the Pacific Fleet. Remember that voyage. She would make it once more.

From her home in California, she deployed to the Western Pacific twenty-two times.

In the summer of nineteen sixty-four, she sailed for the waters off Vietnam. Over the next nine years, she came back again and again, launching strikes from the Gulf of Tonkin.

In nineteen eighty-three, off the Arabian Sea, a fire broke out in one of her main machinery rooms. Fuel from an open valve caught fire. Six sailors died, and thirty-five were hurt.

The date was the first of November. Keep that date in mind.

In nineteen ninety-one, she was in the Persian Gulf for Desert Storm, launching strikes against Iraq.

And at the end of nineteen ninety-two, her aircraft flew over Somalia, as American troops landed there.

That was her last cruise. On the tenth of July, nineteen ninety-three, the Ranger was decommissioned. Her crew walked off, and the flight deck went quiet.

She was towed north to Bremerton, and put into the reserve fleet.

For years, the Navy kept her in case it ever needed her again. It never did.

On the eighth of March, two thousand four, she was struck from the Naval Vessel Register. In plain words, she was no longer a Navy ship.

That opened a door. The Navy put her on its donation list. Any city or group that could take care of her could have her, as a museum.

One group tried. The USS Ranger Foundation, started in two thousand one by former crew and supporters.

Their plan was to bring her to Fairview, Oregon, on the Columbia River, near Portland. A floating museum, open to the public.

The Navy had rules. A new owner had to show it could pay for the first five years of running the ship.

The Foundation's own estimates ran from about ten million dollars to get her ready, up to thirty million in all.

Then there was the river. Moving a carrier that far up the Columbia was a problem of its own.

The Foundation kept trying for years. The money never came.

After eight years on the donation list, the Navy decided it could not keep waiting. In twenty twelve, it took her off the list. She would be scrapped.

Her supporters fought on. But in December, twenty fourteen, the contract came. The penny.

And here is the twist. The Ranger was never sold.

The Navy said it plainly. This is not a sales contract. It is a procurement contract.

In a sale, the buyer pays the Navy. Here, the Navy was the customer. It was paying a company to tow the carrier sixteen thousand miles, and cut her into pieces.

So why only one cent? Because of the steel.

The shipbreaker would keep everything it cut out of her, and sell it. Its price was the cost of the job, minus what it expected that metal to fetch.

For the Ranger, the scrap was worth about as much as the work. So the bid came down to almost nothing.

One cent was the lowest price the Navy could possibly have paid.

And the Ranger was not alone. Her sister ship Forrestal had gone the same way, for one cent, in twenty thirteen. Then the Saratoga, in twenty fourteen. Also one cent.

But the deal is not always that good. Six months before the Ranger, the same company had taken the Constellation, a carrier of the next class. That time, the Navy paid three million dollars.

So the penny was not an insult. It was the best deal the Navy could get.

On the fifth of March, twenty fifteen, tugs pulled the Ranger away from her pier in Bremerton.

People lined the shore to watch. Some of them had served on board.

She was too big for the Panama Canal. So there was only one way to Texas. All the way around South America.

Down the Pacific coast of two continents. Around the southern tip, where she had passed in nineteen fifty-eight, as a brand new ship.

Then north, up the Atlantic, and into the Gulf of Mexico.

About sixteen thousand miles. For four months, a ship that once launched jets moved at the speed of the tug in front of her.

On the twelfth of July, twenty fifteen, after one hundred and twenty-nine days, she reached the channel at Brownsville. Crowds stood on the jetties at South Padre Island to see her come in.

She was the second carrier to arrive that year. The Constellation was already there.

Then the cutting began.

It took just over two years. More than fifty-six thousand tons of her were cut up and recycled.

On the morning of the first of November, twenty seventeen, the last section of the Ranger came out.

The first of November. Thirty-four years to the day after the fire in her machinery room.

At nine fifty that morning, the workers in Brownsville stopped for a moment of silence. For the six sailors who died on board.

So that is what happened to the carrier from the movies. The Navy paid a penny to make her disappear.

And the ship itself is gone.

But not all of her. More than five tons of her fittings went to the USS Lexington Museum, in Corpus Christi, Texas.

Gauges, windows and controls from the machinery room where the six men died went to her veterans, for a memorial.

And some of her brass was turned into dog tags.

Her sisters went the same way. Forrestal. Saratoga. Independence. Not one ship of her class was saved.

So here is the question. Should the Navy have found a way to keep one of them? Or was one cent the right price for a carrier nobody could afford to keep?
