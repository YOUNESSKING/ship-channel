Right now, two hundred and twelve feet under the Gulf of Mexico, lies an aircraft carrier.

No enemy sank her. The United States Navy did. On purpose.

Navy demolition divers set off twenty-two charges inside the USS Oriskany. Thirty-seven minutes later, she was gone.

Getting her ready to sink took over three years and about twenty million dollars. So why pay millions to put a carrier on the sea floor? Why not keep her? Why not scrap her?

The answer is buried in her past. And in what was still inside her when she sank. And it matters right now. On November twelfth, the ocean liner SS United States is scheduled to follow her down. About thirteen nautical miles away.

Start with the day her service ended. In September nineteen seventy-six, the Navy decommissioned her. She was laid up at Bremerton, Washington, in a row of silent grey ships.

For thirteen years, she just waited. In July nineteen eighty-nine, she was struck from the Navy list. On paper, she was no longer a warship.

A ship like that usually ends one of two ways. A museum, or a scrapyard. Four of her sisters were already museums.

Then, in the early nineteen nineties, an offer came from across the Pacific. Japanese investors wanted her as the centrepiece of an attraction in Tokyo Bay. They called it City of America. An American warship, as a theme park in Japan. The money never came. And nobody else asked for her.

So the Navy tried the scrapyard. In the early nineteen nineties, she was sold for scrap. The buyer defaulted. She never even left Navy custody.

In September nineteen ninety-five, a start-up scrap firm called Pegasus bought her for just over a million dollars. The Navy had appraised her at six hundred thousand. Three companies had bid. The deal said she had to be cut up in the United States. A losing bidder protested, and the work was held up until that December.

In nineteen ninety-six, she was towed down from Bremerton to San Francisco Bay. Pegasus planned to break her up at the old Mare Island yard. A year later, almost nothing had been cut. The Navy ended the contract for default and took her back.

Two scrap deals had collapsed. Two more attempts to sell her found no taker at all.

So in nineteen ninety-nine, she was towed out of California. All the way around South America. To a reserve fleet at Beaumont, Texas. A ship nobody would take, dragged around a continent, to wait some more.

So what kind of ship ends up like this? To understand what was being thrown away, go back to the war she was built for. The one she missed.

Her keel was laid in Brooklyn in May nineteen forty-four. She was launched on the thirteenth of October, nineteen forty-five. Six weeks after Japan surrendered. A Second World War carrier that never fought in the Second World War.

In August nineteen forty-seven, the work stopped. Then, in June nineteen fifty, war broke out in Korea, and the Navy rushed her to completion. She was the last Essex-class carrier ever finished. Her crews called her the Mighty O.

On the eighteenth of November, nineteen fifty-two, four of her Panther jets met seven MiG-fifteens. One pilot, Royce Williams, was credited with one MiG at the time. In twenty twenty-three, the Navy cited him for downing four. It upgraded his Silver Star to the Navy Cross.

Then came Vietnam. Between May and December nineteen sixty-five, she flew more than twelve thousand combat sorties. A record for one carrier deployment at the time.

On the ninth of September, nineteen sixty-five, the man who led her air wing was shot down. Commander James Stockdale's Skyhawk was hit by anti-aircraft fire over North Vietnam. He was held prisoner for seven and a half years.

A year later, the danger came from inside the ship. On the morning of the twenty-sixth of October, nineteen sixty-six, she was on Yankee Station in the Gulf of Tonkin. Two sailors were stowing magnesium parachute flares. One went off by accident.

In a panic, a sailor threw the burning flare into a locker packed with about seven hundred more. They burn at about five thousand four hundred degrees Fahrenheit. The fire burned for about seven hours.

Most of the dead were officers, many of them pilots, trapped in their staterooms by the smoke. The fire killed forty-four men. Dozens more were hurt.

Eleven days later, her crew stood in formation for a burial at sea.

She was repaired in San Francisco and was back off Vietnam the next summer. On the twenty-sixth of October, nineteen sixty-seven, she launched a strike on Hanoi. One year to the day after the fire.

One of the pilots was Lieutenant Commander John McCain. A surface-to-air missile tore the wing off his A-four Skyhawk. He came down in a lake in the middle of the city. He was released in March nineteen seventy-three, after five and a half years.

Two of America's best-known prisoners of war had flown from her deck. Forty-four of her men had died in a single morning. And by the late nineteen nineties, nobody would pay to keep her, or even to cut her up. Then, in two thousand three, Congress changed the rules.

In November two thousand three, Congress gave the Navy the power to donate old warships to states as reefs. The Oriskany was the first warship the Navy offered.

Four bids came in, from five states. Florida, Mississippi, Texas, and Georgia with South Carolina. In April two thousand four, the Navy picked Florida as the best value. The plan was to sink her before the end of that summer. It would take two more years.

First, she had to be cleaned. The work was done in Corpus Christi, Texas, and took eleven months. Congress had committed two point eight million dollars to the project. Two point one million of it was for removing contaminants.

Then workers stripped the asphalt off her flight deck. The wood underneath was full of PCBs. The Navy blamed fluid from her catapults. Under the cleanup rules, material that contaminated had to come out, unless the EPA agreed otherwise. So the whole wooden deck came out.

The cost of removing contaminants grew to more than seven point six million dollars. At least half of it went on that deck. On top of that, the Navy spent three point seven million on a computer model. Its job was to prove the PCBs left aboard were safe.

From Texas, she was towed to Pensacola. Then came hurricane season. In June two thousand five, she was towed back to Texas to hide from it. That tow cost another eight hundred thousand dollars. She rode out Hurricane Rita at Beaumont.

By the spring of two thousand six, the Navy's bill was twelve point seven million dollars, and rising. Press reports on sinking day put the total at about twenty million. Florida's own legislature later put it at about twenty-one million, almost all of it Navy money.

That is about seven times what Congress first committed. And the flight deck was not the only place the PCBs were hiding.

On the twenty-second of March, two thousand six, she came back to Pensacola for the last time. She was the test case for new national cleanup rules for ships sunk as reefs.

In May, a Navy bomb-disposal team went deep inside her hull. On the fifteenth, tugs pulled her away from the pier. Former sailors lined the shore to watch her go. One of them stood on the seawall and saluted.

On the morning of the seventeenth of May, she sat at anchor more than twenty miles out in the Gulf. The team had placed twenty-two charges on pipes and valves deep in her hull. More than five hundred pounds of explosives.

At ten twenty-five in the morning, they fired. The Navy expected her to take about five hours to sink. She took thirty-seven minutes.

She went down stern first, her bow rising into the air. Then she was gone. She settled upright on the bottom, her bow pointing due south. Two hundred and twelve feet down.

The moment she touched bottom, she belonged to Florida. She was the largest ship ever deliberately sunk as a reef. Guinness World Records lists her as the largest artificial reef in the world.

It looked like the perfect ending. But her sinking came with a condition. And Florida had to keep checking it.

Not all of the PCBs came out. An estimated seven hundred pounds stayed aboard. They were locked in electrical cable, gaskets, insulation and paint. All the liquid PCBs had been removed.

The EPA reviewed the Navy's studies. On the fifteenth of February, two thousand six, it ruled. Sinking her with the PCBs aboard would not pose an unreasonable risk. It was the first approval of its kind ever given to a ship. And the EPA said it applied to her alone.

There was a condition. Florida had to keep catching fish on the wreck and testing them. From December two thousand six to November two thousand ten, the state and Escambia County sampled eight rounds of fish. Two hundred and fifty-four fish from the wreck.

The first results were not what anyone wanted. For the first two years, the average PCB level in those fish was above two lines. Florida's screening value. And the EPA's.

Screening values are not poison limits. They are the levels at which health officials take a closer look. The highest readings were in red snapper and porgies. Vermilion snapper and scamp never crossed either line.

Environmental groups, using the state's own data, went further. They said snapper on the carrier carried about seven times more PCBs than snapper on a rubble reef. That reef was eight miles away. And tagging suggested the snapper tend to stay on the wreck.

Then the numbers came down. By about three years, the average was below Florida's line. By about four years, it was below both. No fish-eating advisory was ever issued for the wreck.

The last published results stop there, about four years after the sinking. So here is the question the fish leave behind. If the Navy knew the PCBs were there, why not just take them out?

To answer that, go back down to the wreck. She does not sit the way she did on the day she sank. The storms saw to that.

Within about two and a half years, storms had pushed her roughly ten feet down into the seabed. Among them was Hurricane Gustav, in two thousand eight. In late two thousand nine, a storm tore away the casing around her funnel.

Today her flight deck lies about one hundred and forty-five to one hundred and fifty feet down. The top of her island is eighty-four feet down. The deck is beyond normal sport-diving limits. Florida warns divers never to go inside.

The divers came anyway. In her first year, there were about four thousand two hundred charter dive trips. They brought about three point six million dollars of economic impact to two counties. Escambia, in Florida, and Baldwin, in Alabama. And sixty-seven jobs.

Now back to the question. Why leave the PCBs in? Almost all of what was left, ninety-seven percent, was in her wiring. The Navy said ripping it all out would mean taking the ship apart.

And taking her apart is exactly what nobody had managed to do. Two scrap deals had collapsed. Two more had found no taker. Getting the wiring out meant doing the very job that had already failed.

A reef was different. The Navy said reefing and cleanup cost about a quarter of taking a ship apart and recycling it. Even as the bill climbed, the Navy still called her a bargain. Next to an estimated twenty-four million dollars to scrap her.

But that bargain would not hold for long. Within two years, the government's own ship-disposal agency gave a warning. Cleaning ships to the new standard could wipe out the savings of reefing compared to scrapping.

So that is why the Navy sank its own aircraft carrier. Nobody would take her whole. Nobody could make a scrap deal stick. And the PCBs were in her wiring, where only taking her apart could reach them. The bottom of the Gulf was the one door left. By the Navy's own sums, it was also the cheapest.

She is still the only aircraft carrier ever sunk as a reef. She will not be the biggest for long.

On November twelfth, at ten in the morning, weather permitting, Okaloosa County plans to sink the SS United States. She is nine hundred and ninety feet long. No explosives this time. They will flood her. She will lie about thirteen nautical miles from the Oriskany.

And here is the twist. Okaloosa County put fifty thousand dollars toward sinking the Oriskany. Now it is about to take her title.
