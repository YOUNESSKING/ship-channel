# FACT-CHECK B: the reef decision, the cost, the PCBs, the fish (USS Oriskany)

Independent checker B, 4 Nov 2026. Scope: decision to reef, Navy reefing program, cost figures, overrun drivers,
PCBs left aboard, EPA risk-based approval, FWC fish monitoring. Checked against primary documents (EPA releases and
the EPA/MARAD BMP PDF, NAVSEA fact sheet, Navy NewsStand releases, MARAD reports to Congress, FWC 2011 report,
Florida Senate/House bill analyses, FDOH 2025 advisory guide, Congress.gov, USNI, AP, BBC). The CBD/BAN 2012
petition was read in full but is treated as advocacy (its tables use FWC data).

Fact IDs (E4, F2, I3 ...) refer to FACT_SHEET.md.

## Key findings in one paragraph

- **The EPA "$15.63M vs $23.6M" mystery is solved.** $8.28M + $3.61M + $3.74M = **$15.63M exactly**: the three
  "BMP-related" lines (ship cleanup, flight-deck cleanup, PCB risk model). Add towing/berthing ($3.07M) and scuttling
  ($4.90M) and you get **$23.6M**. The document does not "disagree with itself" in its numbers. Its sentence simply
  calls the cleanup subtotal the "total". Do not present this as a contradiction.
- **The wooden flight deck and the catapult-fluid PCBs are one story, not two.** The Navy (Dec 2004) says the wooden
  deck and underlayment were removed "due to high PCB concentrations found after the asphalt protective coating had
  been removed". USNI says the elevated PCBs came from catapult fluid.
- **"First" is confirmed (Navy release). "One-off" is confirmed (EPA: "applies only to the ex-Oriskany").** I found no
  later reef ship sunk under a risk-based PCB approval, but I cannot prove there is none. Say "first", not "only ever".
- **Fish levels were above *screening values*, and no consumption advisory was ever issued.** The FDOH 2025 advisory
  guide has no Oriskany entry. The claim that FDOH nearly issued one in 2010 comes only from the advocacy petition.

## Safe narration wording (health and environment)

1. "The EPA let her sink with about 700 pounds of PCBs still sealed in her wiring, gaskets and paint, after ruling
   that it would 'not pose an unreasonable risk'."
2. "It was the first approval of its kind ever given to a ship, and the EPA said it applied to her alone."
3. "There was a condition: Florida had to keep catching fish on the wreck and testing them."
4. "For the first two years, the average PCB level in those fish was above both Florida's and the EPA's screening
   values. Those are the levels at which health officials take a closer look."
5. "The highest readings were in red snapper and porgies. Vermilion snapper and scamp never crossed either line."
6. "Then the numbers came down. By about four years after the sinking, the average was below both lines."
7. "No fish-eating advisory was ever issued for the wreck."
8. "Environmental groups, using the state's data, said snapper on the carrier carried about seven times more PCBs
   than snapper on a rubble reef eight miles away." (Attributed. Same-day 2008 samples.)
9. Optional, attributed: "Those groups say Florida's health department weighed a warning in 2010, but the oil spill
   closed the fishery first."
10. Cost: "Congress had committed under $3 million. The final bill was around $20 million. About $15.6 million of
    that went just on making her clean enough to sink, including ripping out a wooden flight deck soaked in PCBs."

### Do NOT say
- "Poisoned fish" / "toxic fish" / "unsafe to eat" (no advisory was issued; the values are screening levels).
- "The only ship ever approved to sink with PCBs" (unproven; say "first").
- "The EPA's own document contradicts itself" (the $15.63M is the cleanup subtotal).
- "Florida banned ship sinkings after this" (wrong).
- "Levels are back to normal today" / "the fish are safe now" (no published data after ~4 years found).
- "+1,446%" on screen without attribution (advocacy arithmetic on mixed baselines).
- "Florida's PCB limit for safe consumption" (AP wording; FWC calls it a screening value).
