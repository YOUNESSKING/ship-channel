# HOOK FACTS: "Why the Navy Sank Its Own Aircraft Carrier"

Raw material for a twist about every 90 s. Ranked by surprise × how sure we are. Fact IDs refer to FACT_SHEET.md.
CLAUDE.md gates still apply: no ship résumé in the first 2 min, and the answer is held to the last act.

## Top 15 (ranked)

1. **She went down with ~700 lb of toxic PCBs still on board, and the EPA signed off.** It was the first risk-based
   PCB disposal approval ever given for a reef ship, and a one-off. (E7, E10) HIGH
2. **Then the fish proved the model wrong.** For the first two years, average PCB levels in fish caught on the wreck
   were above both Florida's and the EPA's safety screening values. The red snapper average went from about 2 ppb before
   the sinking to about 54 ppb after. (I1, I3) HIGH / MED. *Pay-off: levels fell below both lines within about 4 years.*
3. **Under $3 million budgeted, about $20 million spent.** Congress committed $2.8M. The final figure was ~$20M, and
   the EPA's own document disagrees with itself ($15.63M in the text, $23.6M in its table). (E4, F2–F5) HIGH/MED
4. **The Navy had tried to scrap her twice and to sell her to Japan as a museum, and all of it failed.** One scrapper
   defaulted, the next (a start-up at Mare Island) collapsed, and a plan to show her in Tokyo Bay ran out of money. (D4–D7) HIGH
5. **A 32,000-ton carrier sank in 37 minutes.** The engineers had predicted up to five hours. (G5, G6) HIGH
6. **The hidden cost was the deck itself.** At least half of the extra cleanup money went on ripping out the wooden
   flight deck, which nobody had planned for, plus PCB-laced catapult fluid. (E5) MED
7. **She was a WWII carrier that never fought in WWII.** She was launched two months after Japan surrendered, then left
   half-built for three years until Korea made the Navy finish her. (A4–A6) HIGH
8. **The fire, then McCain, one year apart to the day.** On 26 Oct 1966 a single dropped flare killed 44 men, many of
   them pilots fresh from combat. On 26 Oct 1967 John McCain took off from the same deck and was shot down over Hanoi. (C3, C7, C12) HIGH
9. **Storms pushed her ~10 ft into the seabed, and the flight deck is now too deep for ordinary scuba.** It was meant to
   sit near the 130-ft recreational limit; it is now ~145–150 ft. (H2, H5, H9) HIGH
10. **The county next door helped pay to sink her, and now it is about to beat her.** Okaloosa County gave $50,000 to
    the Oriskany sinking in 2006. On 12 Nov 2026 it plans to sink the 990-ft SS United States about 13 nmi away and take
    the "world's largest artificial reef" title. (F6, J1, J4, J5) MED/HIGH
11. **Before she was a reef, she was a movie star.** William Holden flew aboard to film *The Bridges at Toko-Ri*. (B6) HIGH
12. **A secret dogfight with Soviet MiGs.** On 18 Nov 1952 Oriskany jets fought seven Soviet-flown MiG-15s. The record
    credited one kill to Royce Williams; decades later Congress was told he got four. (B2, B3) HIGH/MED
13. **She spent 30 years as a problem nobody could solve.** Decommissioned 1976, struck 1989, towed from California
    around South America to a Texas reserve fleet in 1999, and then sent back to Texas again in 2005 to hide from
    hurricanes ($800,000 for that tow). (D1, D3, D8, E6) HIGH/MED
14. **The "$92 million a year" promise was not about this ship.** The Navy and BBC quoted it on sinking day, but it was
    a study of all of Escambia County's reefs. The first real year brought ~4,200 dive trips and $3.6M of impact. (H10–H12) HIGH
15. **Florida took legal ownership at the exact moment she touched bottom.** (G12) MED

Reserve (needs verifying before use): Stockdale also flew from her (C1, HIGH, a good "two famous POWs" pairing); the
Florida health department nearly issued a snapper warning in 2010 but Deepwater Horizon closed the fishery first (I8,
UNVERIFIED); a Hollywood "Hell" film set (D10, UNVERIFIED).

## 3 mid-video reversal angles

**R1: "The perfect reef ... that poisoned its fish." (strongest)** First half: the triumph (37 minutes, upright, divers,
money). Reversal at the midpoint: FWC's own monitoring finds PCBs in the snapper above safety lines, and the EPA model
was wrong. Final act: the numbers fall again, so was it a success or a toxic mistake? It sets up the closing
question about the SS United States. Sources: FWC11, CBD12, AP 2012, EPAREL.

**R2: "Cheap disposal that cost seven times the budget."** The reef was sold as the bargain option ($2.8M versus $12–24M to
scrap). Reversal: the wooden deck, the catapult PCBs, the hurricane tows and a $3.74M risk model take it to ~$20M, in the
same range as scrapping. Then a second turn: the tourism pays it back? (First-year impact was $3.6M, so the payback is
measured in years, not the "$92M a year" promised.) Sources: USNI06, EPABMP, FLSEN, EDIS.

**R3: "The Navy didn't choose to sink her; it ran out of every other option."** The title promise ("why") seems to have a simple
answer (a reef for tourism). Reversal: the real reason is that a museum, two scrap contracts and a reactivation all
failed, and an unfunded, rusting carrier was costing money in Texas. The reef was the last door left. It ties directly to
the SS United States, which also went to reef only after a museum plan and a pier eviction failed. Sources: DANFS, GSEC,
LAT06a, FINDLAW, MAREX 2024.
