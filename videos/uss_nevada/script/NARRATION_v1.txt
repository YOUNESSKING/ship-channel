On the first of July, nineteen forty-six, the United States dropped an atomic bomb on one of its own battleships.

The Navy had painted her bright orange, so the bomber could not miss. The bomb missed her by about seven hundred yards. When the smoke cleared, she was still floating.

Three weeks later, they tried again. A second atomic bomb, this time under the water, about a thousand yards away. It sank a battleship and an aircraft carrier nearby. She was still floating.

This is the USS Nevada. The battleship that survived two atomic bombs.

It was not the first time she refused to die. Five years earlier, at Pearl Harbor, she was the only battleship that got under way.

So if two atomic bombs could not sink her, what did? And where is she now? For more than seventy years, nobody could say.

Start with the place. Bikini Atoll, in the Marshall Islands, in the middle of the Pacific. A ring of small islands around a lagoon about twenty miles long.

In the summer of nineteen forty-six, that lagoon held a fleet. About ninety target ships, anchored on purpose where an atomic bomb would fall.

The operation was called Crossroads. Its question was simple. What does an atomic bomb do to a navy?

About forty-two thousand men took part, with two hundred and forty-two ships and one hundred and fifty-six aircraft. Cameras were set up on towers, on islands, and in planes.

The targets were old American warships, captured German and Japanese ships, and small craft. Many carried animals. Goats, pigs and rats were placed on board to see what the blast would do to a living crew.

At the centre of it all was one ship. The Navy chose her as the bull's-eye. The point the bomber would aim at.

They painted her international orange, with white trim, so she would stand out from every other ship in the lagoon. At night, a flashing light on board marked her for the aircrews.

On the twenty-eighth of May, she had sailed into the lagoon with a crew of four hundred and three. On the thirtieth of June, the crew left her. Behind them, in her pilot house, they left goats and rats.

Why this ship? Why make the Nevada the target? To understand what the Navy was aiming at, go back to a Sunday morning in Hawaii.

Pearl Harbor. The seventh of December, nineteen forty-one. Along the east side of Ford Island lay the Pacific Fleet's battleships, most of them moored in pairs.

The Nevada lay alone, at the end of the line, just astern of the USS Arizona.

At about one minute past eight, Japanese planes came over the harbour. Within minutes, a torpedo tore into the Nevada's port bow.

Her captain was ashore. So was her executive officer. The senior officer on board was a reserve lieutenant commander named Francis Thomas.

Then the Arizona blew up. Burning oil spread across the water and drifted toward the Nevada.

Staying meant burning with the Arizona. So the crew did something no other battleship managed that morning. They got her moving.

A battleship normally needs tugs and hours of preparation to leave her berth. The Nevada had neither. Her engineers raised steam on their own.

Chief Boatswain Edwin Hill led a party onto the quay under fire. They cast off her lines. Then Hill swam back out to the ship.

At about twenty to nine, without tugs, the Nevada backed away from her berth and headed down the channel toward the open sea.

A single battleship, on fire, moving past the wreckage of the fleet.

The Japanese saw her too. In the second wave, dive bombers went for her.

She was hit by at least five bombs. Her forward decks were on fire. Water was pouring in.

Her officers saw the danger. If she sank in the narrow channel, she could trap the whole fleet inside Pearl Harbor.

So they ran her aground. At about ten past nine, she went into the mud near Hospital Point.

On the forecastle, Chief Boatswain Hill was trying to let go the anchors when bombs exploded around him. He was killed. He was awarded the Medal of Honor.

Below decks, Machinist Donald Ross kept her electric plant running until he was blinded and knocked unconscious. Twice. He also received the Medal of Honor.

Later that morning, tugs helped pull her free. Then her crew beached her a second time, stern first, on the other side of the channel, at Waipio Point. The channel stayed open.

Around sixty of her men were killed. Over a hundred were wounded. But the Nevada had done what no other battleship did that day. She had fought back, and she had moved.

By the next day, she had settled onto the bottom in shallow water. For most ships, that would have been the end of the story.

Divers spent more than fifteen hundred hours inside her flooded hull. Two salvage men died from poison gas that had built up in her compartments.

On the twelfth of February, nineteen forty-two, the Nevada floated again. In April she sailed for Puget Sound, on the West Coast, to be rebuilt.

She came back to the war in nineteen forty-three, off the frozen island of Attu, in the Aleutians.

Then she crossed to the Atlantic. On the sixth of June, nineteen forty-four, she was off Utah Beach in Normandy, firing on German positions ahead of the landings.

Of all the battleships at Pearl Harbor that morning, the Nevada was the only one at D-Day.

German shore batteries fired back. The Navy's own history says they straddled her twenty-seven times. She was not hit.

On the twenty-fifth of June, she shelled the fortress port of Cherbourg. In August, she moved to the Mediterranean and duelled the heavy guns at Toulon, in southern France.

Then she went back to the Pacific. In February nineteen forty-five, she was off Iwo Jima.

In March, off Okinawa, a kamikaze crashed into her deck near her number three turret. Eleven men were killed.

Days later, a shore battery hit her. Two more were killed. She kept firing.

She finished the war with seven battle stars. She was also one of the oldest battleships the Navy still had. Launched in nineteen fourteen. Built for a different kind of war.

When the war ended, the Navy had more battleships than it needed. Old ones were going to the scrapyard.

The Nevada had survived a torpedo, bombs, a beaching, a kamikaze and shore batteries on two oceans. That made her exactly what the Crossroads planners wanted. A tough old ship to test the most powerful weapon ever made.

The first of July, nineteen forty-six. Test Able.

At Kwajalein, about two hundred and fifty miles away, a B-29 bomber named Dave's Dream took off with the bomb.

The Nevada's own crew watched from a transport about twenty-one miles away.

At thirty-four seconds past nine in the morning, the bomb went off, about five hundred and twenty feet above the lagoon.

Its force was about twenty-three thousand tons of TNT. The same type of bomb that destroyed Nagasaki less than a year earlier.

But the bomb did not explode over the Nevada. It went off about seven hundred yards away. Almost on top of the transport Gilliam.

Nobody ever found out exactly why it missed.

Five ships sank. The Gilliam and the transport Carlisle went down first. Then the destroyers Lamson and Anderson. The next day, the Japanese cruiser Sakawa followed them.

The Nevada was hit by the blast and the heat. Both of her topmasts came down. Her superstructure was dished in. Fires broke out on her decks. Her boilers were put out of action.

But her hull was almost untouched. And in her pilot house, the Navy's inspectors found the goats and rats. They appeared unaffected.

The orange ship had taken an atomic bomb, and she was still floating.

To the public, the first test looked like a disappointment. The atomic bomb had not wiped out the fleet. So the second test would be different.

The twenty-fifth of July, nineteen forty-six. Test Baker.

This time the bomb was not dropped. It hung under a landing ship, ninety feet beneath the surface of the lagoon.

At about eight thirty-five in the morning, it went off. Another twenty-three kilotons.

A column of water and steam rose more than a mile into the air. Millions of tons of seawater lifted out of the lagoon, then fell back onto the fleet.

The old battleship Arkansas was about two hundred and forty yards away. She went down. The aircraft carrier Saratoga, about three hundred and eighty yards away, sank that afternoon. Two submarines went down too. Four days later, the Japanese battleship Nagato sank in the night.

The Nevada was about a thousand and sixty yards from the bomb. The shock hit her hull. Some compartments aft flooded. She took a slight list.

And she was still floating.

But this time, surviving was the problem. The water that fell on the fleet carried the bomb's radiation.

The Navy's inspectors called the Nevada highly radioactive, inside and outside.

Sailors tried to wash the target ships down with hoses, with soap and with lye. It did not work well enough.

The Nevada had survived two atomic bombs. Now nobody could live on her.

On the twenty-ninth of August, nineteen forty-six, she was decommissioned. The ship that refused to sink was still afloat. But her life as a warship was over.

The Navy studied her for almost two more years. In May nineteen forty-seven, she was towed to Pearl Harbor, the same harbour she had escaped from, for radiological tests.

Then the Navy decided how she would end. She would be one last target.

On the twenty-sixth of July, nineteen forty-eight, a tug towed her out to sea, southwest of Oahu.

First, new secret explosives were tested on her. They ripped holes in her sides. She did not sink.

Days of bombs and rockets followed. She was still floating.

On the thirty-first of July, the cruisers Astoria and Pasadena opened fire. Then the battleship Iowa joined them.

In about twenty minutes, the three ships fired two hundred and eighty-seven shells at her. Twenty-seven of them came from the Iowa's sixteen-inch guns.

The Nevada was burning. She was still floating.

In the end, it took aerial torpedoes. Navy planes put torpedoes into her side, below the waterline.

At about two thirty-five in the afternoon, she rolled over and sank.

The admiral who watched her go said, "She was a grand old ship."

She had survived Pearl Harbor. Two oceans of war. Two atomic bombs. In the end, it took four days, three warships and aircraft from her own navy to sink her.

And with her went three guns from her old neighbour on Battleship Row. Years earlier, three of the USS Arizona's fourteen-inch guns had been fitted into the Nevada. They went down with her.

So that is what sank her. Her own navy.

But for more than seventy years, nobody knew exactly where she lay. Ships' logs from nineteen forty-eight gave only rough positions. The water there is nearly three miles deep.

In twenty sixteen, a researcher with NOAA went back to those logs, the logs of the Iowa, the Astoria and the Pasadena, and worked out where the Nevada had gone down.

In twenty twenty, the survey ship Pacific Constructor searched that area with an underwater robot. It was run by two private companies, Ocean Infinity and SEARCH.

In May twenty twenty, they announced it. They had found the Nevada.

She lies about sixty-five nautical miles southwest of Pearl Harbor, more than fifteen thousand four hundred feet down.

She is upside down. Her bow and her stern have broken away. Two of her turrets lie on the sea floor, upside down too.

On her stern, the number thirty-six can still be read. In places, traces of orange paint survive.

The ship that survived Pearl Harbor and two atomic bombs is now protected by law, as a United States Navy wreck.

Donald Ross, the machinist who kept her lights on at Pearl Harbor, died in nineteen ninety-two. His ashes were scattered over her grave. They were carried there by a submarine. The USS Nevada.

Many people believe she should have been saved, as a museum, like the Missouri. Instead, the Navy used her as a target until she sank.
