Almost twenty-three thousand feet under the Philippine Sea, there is a small American warship that once charged a fleet of battleships.

She lies deeper than any shipwreck ever found. Nearly twice as deep as the Titanic.

She was one of the smallest warships in the battle. A destroyer escort, built for chasing submarines. And on one October morning in nineteen forty-four, she was sent straight at the biggest guns in the world.

This is the USS Samuel B. Roberts.

Her crew called her the Sammy B. Most of them never expected to come home that day.

So how did a ship this small end up this deep? And what did the cameras find, when someone finally reached her?

Start with the place. The east coast of Samar, an island in the central Philippines, on the twenty-fifth of October, nineteen forty-four.

Just after dawn, a small American group was steaming off the coast. Its radio call sign was Taffy Three.

Six small escort carriers. Three destroyers. Four destroyer escorts. Their job was to support the troops landing on Leyte, to the south. Not to fight a fleet.

At about a quarter to seven, lookouts saw tall masts on the northern horizon. Then the shape of battleships.

It was the Japanese Center Force, under Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita. Four battleships. Six heavy cruisers. Two light cruisers. And more than ten destroyers.

They had come through the San Bernardino Strait in the night. The big American battleships and fleet carriers that should have stopped them had gone north, chasing a decoy.

Nobody was in the way. Except Taffy Three.

At the head of the Japanese line was the Yamato. The largest battleship ever built. More than eight hundred and sixty feet long, with nine guns of eighteen inches.

Within minutes, her shells were landing among the American carriers. They came down in colours: the Japanese marked their shells with dye, so each ship could see its own splashes.

The Samuel B. Roberts was about three hundred feet long. Her biggest guns were five inches across. Yamato's shells were more than three times wider.

On paper, it was not a fight. It was a massacre waiting to happen.

To understand what the Roberts did next, go back six months, to a shipyard in Houston, Texas.

She was a John C. Butler class destroyer escort, built by Brown Shipbuilding, and commissioned on the twenty-eighth of April, nineteen forty-four.

Destroyer escorts were the Navy's cheap ships. Small, slow by warship standards, and built fast, to guard convoys and hunt submarines. They were never meant to face a battleship.

She carried two five-inch guns, three torpedoes and depth charges. And a crew of a little over two hundred men, most of them young, and many of them at sea for the first time.

Her name came from a coxswain. Samuel Booker Roberts was a Navy landing-craft driver at Guadalcanal. In September, nineteen forty-two, he steered his boat between Japanese guns and a group of trapped Marines, to draw the fire away while they were taken off the beach.

He was killed. He was twenty-one. He received the Navy Cross.

Her captain was Lieutenant Commander Robert Copeland. Not a career officer. He was a lawyer from Tacoma, Washington, and a Naval Reserve officer.

Six months after she left Houston, his ship was off Samar, with the Japanese fleet coming over the horizon.

The American admiral, Clifton Sprague, did the only things he could. He turned his carriers away, into rain squalls. He launched every plane he had. He ordered the escorts to make smoke.

Then he sent his small ships to attack.

The three destroyers went first. The Johnston, the Hoel and the Heermann. Then the order reached the destroyer escorts.

Copeland spoke to his crew over the loudspeaker. By most accounts, his words were short. We're making a torpedo run. The outcome is doubtful, but we will do our duty.

The Roberts came out of the smoke and headed for a column of Japanese heavy cruisers.

She closed to about four thousand yards. Two miles, from ships many times her size. Close enough that the cruisers' big guns had trouble hitting something so small.

Then she fired all three of her torpedoes at the heavy cruiser Chokai, and turned back into the smoke.

Her crew believed they had scored a hit. That is the story that made her famous.

But here is the twist. Historians who have looked at the evidence since are not so sure that any of those torpedoes hit.

The Chokai was wrecked that morning. But what wrecked her is still argued about. A shell from an American carrier's gun, bombs from the air, or the Roberts herself.

What is not in doubt is what the Roberts did next.

Her torpedoes were gone. She could have stayed in the smoke. Instead, Copeland turned her back towards the enemy, and opened fire with her guns.

For close to an hour, the little ship duelled with heavy cruisers. Her two five-inch guns fired more than six hundred shells. When the regular rounds ran low, her gunners fired star shells, and practice rounds.

She took hits, and kept firing. She was hit again. Then, at about nine o'clock, the shells that finished her arrived.

They came from the battleship Kongo. Fourteen-inch shells, each one heavier than a small car.

They tore a hole in her side about forty feet long, and wrecked her engine room. The Roberts slowed, and stopped.

At her aft gun, Gunner's Mate Paul Carr kept working. The gun's barrel had overheated and exploded, and the mount was wrecked. Carr was found holding the last shell, trying to load it.

He died at the gun. He was nineteen. He received the Silver Star, and the Navy later named a frigate after him, the USS Carr.

The order came to abandon ship. A little after ten in the morning, the Samuel B. Roberts rolled over and went down, stern first.

And then something nobody expected happened.

The Japanese fleet turned around.

Kurita had battleships and cruisers. He was winning. And at about a quarter past nine, he gave the order to break off, and turned his force away to the north.

Why he did it is still debated. His ships were scattered by the smoke and the torpedo attacks. Planes from the escort carriers had been striking his ships all morning. He believed he was fighting a much bigger force than he was.

The small ships had done what they were sent to do. They had made the Japanese fleet believe it was facing far more.

Taffy Three paid for it. The escort carriers Gambier Bay and Saint Lo were sunk. So were the destroyers Johnston and Hoel. And the Samuel B. Roberts.

More than a thousand Americans were killed or missing.

But the landings on Leyte were saved. The Japanese fleet never came that close again.

For the men of the Roberts, the battle was over. The ordeal was not.

About a hundred and twenty survivors were in the water, holding on to three rafts and a few nets. Many were burned. Some were badly wounded.

They drifted for about fifty hours. Under the sun by day, in the cold at night. Sharks circled. Men who had survived the battle died on the rafts.

Help came on the twenty-seventh of October. Landing craft sent out from Leyte found them and picked them up.

Eighty-nine men of the Samuel B. Roberts had been killed.

Copeland received the Navy Cross. Taffy Three received the Presidential Unit Citation. And the Navy remembered the little ship. Later ships were given her name.

But the ship herself was gone. Somewhere off Samar, in some of the deepest water on Earth.

For seventy-seven years, nobody knew exactly where.

The problem was the depth. East of Samar, the sea floor falls away into the Philippine Trench, one of the deepest places in any ocean.

For most of history, a wreck down there was simply out of reach.

That changed in twenty nineteen. An American explorer, Victor Vescovo, had built a submersible that could dive to the bottom of any ocean. It was called the Limiting Factor.

In twenty twenty-one, Vescovo dived on the wreck of the Johnston, another ship from that morning. She lay about twenty-one thousand feet down. The deepest shipwreck ever found.

It was a record. It would last little more than a year.

In June twenty twenty-two, Vescovo came back to Samar, to look for the Roberts.

The old reported position was not good enough. A naval historian on the team, Parks Stephenson, went back through the records, and worked out a better search area.

Sonar found a target on the slope. Then the submersible went down to look.

Over eight days, the team made six dives. On the twenty-second of June, Vescovo piloted the submersible down to her himself.

The Navy confirmed it a few days later. This was the Samuel B. Roberts.

She lies almost seven thousand metres down. Close to twenty-three thousand feet. Deeper than the Johnston. Deeper than any shipwreck ever found.

Guinness World Records lists her as the deepest shipwreck found to date. As of today, no deeper wreck has been found.

So what did the cameras see, down there in the dark?

The Roberts lies in two pieces, close together, on the slope.

The bow buckled when it hit the bottom. The stern has broken away, a few metres from the rest of the hull.

On her side, the number four-one-three can still be read.

Her mast lies where it fell. Her torpedo launcher is still on deck, its three tubes empty. The ones she fired at the Chokai.

The aft gun mount is there. The gun where Paul Carr died.

Her depth charges are still in their racks, never used.

And in her side, the team saw the damage from the battleship's shells. The wounds that sank her, still open, after almost eighty years.

So that is what is down there. A ship that was never meant to fight a battleship, lying exactly as the battle left her.

The Navy calls the site a hallowed war grave. Nothing is to be taken, and no one is to disturb it.

The cameras came, and went. The Roberts stays where she is, with the men who never left her.

So here is the question. Should wrecks this deep be left in the dark forever? Or should the cameras go back, so that more people can see what the Sammy B did?
