Almost twenty-three thousand feet under the Philippine Sea, there is a small American warship that once charged a fleet of battleships.

She is the deepest shipwreck ever found. Nearly twice as deep as the Titanic. Stand Mount Kilimanjaro on the sea floor beside her, and its summit would still be about three thousand feet under the waves.

She was one of the smallest warships in the battle. A destroyer escort, built for chasing submarines. And on one October morning in nineteen forty-four, she was sent straight at the biggest guns in the world.

This is the USS Samuel B. Roberts.

Her crew called her the Sammy B. And that morning, her captain told them not to expect to survive.

So how did a ship this small end up this deep? And what did the cameras find, when someone finally reached her?

Start with the place. The east coast of Samar, an island in the central Philippines, on the twenty-fifth of October, nineteen forty-four.

Just after dawn, a small American group was steaming off the coast. Its radio call sign was Taffy Three.

Six small escort carriers. Three destroyers. Four destroyer escorts. Their job was to support the troops who had landed on Leyte, to the south. Not to fight a fleet.

At about a quarter to seven, lookouts saw tall masts on the northern horizon. Then the shapes of battleships.

It was the Japanese Center Force, under Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita. Four battleships. Six heavy cruisers. And about a dozen destroyers.

They had come through the San Bernardino Strait in the night. The American battleships and fleet carriers that could have stopped them were far to the north, chasing a Japanese decoy.

Nobody stood in the way. Nobody, except Taffy Three.

At the head of the Japanese line was the Yamato. The largest battleship of the war. More than eight hundred and sixty feet long, with nine guns of eighteen inches.

Just before seven, she opened fire. Within minutes, giant shells were landing among the American carriers.

The Samuel B. Roberts was about three hundred feet long. Her biggest guns were five inches across. Yamato's shells were more than three times as wide. And the Yamato weighed more than forty times as much.

On paper, it was not a battle. It was a massacre waiting to happen. The Americans had smoke, rain, planes from the small carriers, and a handful of small ships. That was all.

To understand what the Roberts did next, go back six months, to a shipyard in Houston, Texas.

She was a John C. Butler class destroyer escort, built by Brown Shipbuilding, and commissioned on the twenty-eighth of April, nineteen forty-four.

Destroyer escorts were the Navy's small ships. Built fast and in large numbers, to guard convoys and hunt submarines. They were never meant to face a battleship.

She carried two five-inch guns, smaller anti-aircraft guns, three torpedoes and depth charges. And a crew of about two hundred and twenty men.

Her name came from a young sailor. Samuel Booker Roberts was a coxswain, the driver of a landing boat, at Guadalcanal.

In September, nineteen forty-two, Marines were trapped on a beach at Point Cruz. Roberts took his boat in close, and drew the Japanese fire towards himself, so the other boats could take the Marines off.

He was badly wounded, and died the next day. He was twenty-one. He received the Navy Cross.

Her captain was Lieutenant Commander Robert Copeland. Not a career officer. He was a lawyer from Tacoma, Washington, born there in nineteen ten, who had joined the Naval Reserve in the nineteen thirties.

When the war came, he went to sea. Before the Roberts, he had already commanded another destroyer escort, the Wyman. Now he had a brand new ship of his own.

Six months after she left Houston, his ship was off Samar, with the Japanese fleet coming over the horizon.

The American admiral, Clifton Sprague, did the only things he could. He turned his carriers away and ran. He launched every plane he had. He ordered his escorts to make smoke, to hide the carriers.

Then he sent his small ships to attack. Destroyers and destroyer escorts, against battleships and cruisers.

The three destroyers went first. The Johnston, the Hoel and the Heermann. The Johnston's captain, Ernest Evans, would receive the Medal of Honor. He did not survive the day. Then the order reached the destroyer escorts.

Copeland spoke to his crew over the loudspeaker. He later reported what he told them. This will be a fight against overwhelming odds, from which survival cannot be expected. We will do what damage we can.

At around half past seven, the Roberts came out of the smoke, and headed for a column of Japanese heavy cruisers.

She closed to about four thousand yards. Two miles, from ships many times her size. So close, and so small, that the cruisers' big guns struggled to hit her.

Then she fired all three of her torpedoes at the heavy cruiser Chokai, and turned back into the smoke.

Her crew believed one of them hit. In nineteen forty-four, the Navy said so too, in its own press release. That is the story that made her famous.

But here is the twist. Japanese records do not back it up. Many historians now believe that none of her torpedoes hit.

The Chokai was crippled that morning, and she sank. But what crippled her is still argued about. Bombs from American planes. A shell from one of the escort carriers. Or the guns of the Roberts herself.

What is not in doubt is what the Roberts did next.

Her torpedoes were gone. She could have stayed hidden in the smoke. Instead, Copeland turned her back towards the enemy, and opened fire with her guns.

For close to an hour, the little ship duelled with heavy cruisers. Her two five-inch guns fired more than six hundred shells. One gun alone fired more than three hundred.

She took hits, and kept firing. Then, at about nine o'clock, the shells that finished her arrived.

They came from the battleship Kongo. Fourteen-inch shells, fired as a salvo.

They tore a hole in her side about forty feet long, and wrecked one of her engine rooms. The Roberts slowed, and stopped. The ship that had charged a fleet was now dead in the water, still under fire.

At one of her guns, Gunner's Mate Third Class Paul Carr kept working. A powder charge had exploded inside the gun, and he was mortally wounded.

His Silver Star citation says that he still tried, alone, to load the last shell into the gun. He died there.

He came from Checotah, Oklahoma. Years later, the Navy named a frigate after him. The USS Carr.

Shortly after nine, the order came to abandon ship.

A little after ten in the morning, the Samuel B. Roberts went down.

And then something nobody expected happened.

The Japanese fleet turned around.

Kurita had battleships and cruisers. On paper, he was winning. And at about twenty past nine, he broke off the attack, and turned his force away to the north.

Why he did it is still debated. His ships were scattered by the smoke, and by the torpedo attacks. Planes from the escort carriers had been striking them all morning. He believed he had been fighting fleet carriers, a much bigger force than he was.

By the time the Roberts went down, the fleet she had charged was already turning away.

The small ships had done what they were sent to do. They had made the Japanese fleet believe it was facing far more than it was.

Taffy Three paid for it. The escort carriers Gambier Bay and Saint Lo were sunk. The Saint Lo was hit by a kamikaze. So were the destroyers Johnston and Hoel. And the Samuel B. Roberts.

More than a thousand Americans were killed or missing. But the Japanese fleet never reached the landing beaches at Leyte.

For the men of the Roberts, the battle was over. The ordeal was not.

About a hundred and twenty survivors were in the water, holding on to three rafts. Many were burned. Some were badly wounded.

They drifted for about fifty hours. More than two days. Under the sun by day, in the cold at night. Sharks circled the rafts. Men who had lived through the battle died in the water.

At last, a patrol craft and a group of landing craft found them, and picked them up.

About ninety men of the Samuel B. Roberts were dead. Out of a crew of about two hundred and twenty.

Copeland survived. After the war, he went back to the law in Tacoma. He later told the story of his ship in a short book, The Spirit of the Sammy B.

Copeland received the Navy Cross. Taffy Three received the Presidential Unit Citation, for extraordinary heroism against powerful units of the Japanese fleet.

And the Navy kept her name alive. A destroyer was named Samuel B. Roberts after the war. Then a frigate. In nineteen eighty-eight, that frigate struck a mine in the Persian Gulf. Her crew fought the flooding and the fires, and saved her. No one was killed. Four days later, the Navy struck back at Iranian oil platforms and warships, in an operation called Praying Mantis.

But the first Samuel B. Roberts was gone. Somewhere off Samar, in some of the deepest water on Earth.

For almost seventy-eight years, nobody knew exactly where.

The problem was the depth. Off the coast of Samar, the sea floor drops away to depths that most ships, and most submarines, could never reach.

At that depth, the water presses down with roughly seven hundred times the pressure at the surface. More than ten thousand pounds on every square inch. It is completely dark, and close to freezing.

For most of history, a wreck down there was simply out of reach. Divers could never go there. Most research submarines could not either.

That changed with one man. Victor Vescovo, an American explorer, with a submersible built to dive to the deepest parts of any ocean. It was called the Limiting Factor.

In March, twenty twenty-one, Vescovo took it down to the wreck of the Johnston, another ship from that morning. Two years earlier, an unmanned search from the research ship Petrel had seen wreckage from her, about twenty thousand feet down. Vescovo went deeper, and found the rest of her. About twenty-one thousand feet down. The deepest shipwreck ever found.

It was a record. It lasted about fifteen months.

In June, twenty twenty-two, Vescovo came back to Samar, with the expedition company EYOS, to look for the Roberts.

The position in the old records was not good enough. A naval historian on the team, Parks Stephenson, went back through the reports, and worked out a better place to search.

A sonar specialist, Jeremie Morizet, searched the slope. Sonar found a target. Then the submersible went down to look.

Over eight days, the team made six dives. Each one was a trip of about four miles down, and four miles back up. On the twenty-second of June, Vescovo piloted the submersible down to her himself.

The Navy confirmed it a few days later. This was the Samuel B. Roberts. The expedition handed its images and data to the Navy's historians, and kept the exact position private.

She lies almost six thousand nine hundred metres down. About four miles. Deeper than the Johnston, by more than four hundred metres.

The Titanic lies about twelve thousand five hundred feet down. The Roberts is nearly twice as deep.

Guinness World Records lists her as the deepest shipwreck found to date. The dives to reach her were the deepest ever made to a shipwreck by a crewed vessel. In June twenty twenty-two, she became the deepest shipwreck ever located. And she still holds the record.

So what did the cameras see, down there in the dark?

The Roberts lies in two pieces, close together, on the slope.

Her stern has broken away from the rest of the hull.

Her triple torpedo launcher is still on deck. Its torpedoes were fired that October morning, at the Chokai.

One of her gun mounts is there too, with its breech broken.

And in her hull, the team saw the damage from the battleship's shells. The wound that sank her, still open, after almost eighty years.

So that is what is down there. A ship that was never meant to fight a battleship, lying where the battle left her.

Three hundred feet of steel, built in Houston in a few months. Two small guns, and three torpedoes. Sent against the largest battleship of the war, and a fleet of cruisers. And for almost eighty years, nobody could reach her.

The Navy's director of naval history, Samuel Cox, put it plainly. The site of the wreck marks the location of a hallowed war grave. Under American law, no one may disturb it without permission. The penalty can reach a hundred thousand dollars a day.

The cameras came, and went. The Roberts stays where she is, with the men who never left her.

And she may not be alone down there. The Gambier Bay, sunk that same morning, has never been found. Two dives below seven thousand metres failed to reach her. If she is ever found, she may be deeper still.

So here is the question. Should the search go on, to find the last ships of Taffy Three? Or should wrecks this deep be left in the dark, with the men who went down with them?
