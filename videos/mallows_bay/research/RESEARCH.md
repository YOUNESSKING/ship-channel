## A. Location and the "30 miles" in the title

- Mallows Bay is a small bay on the Maryland (east) bank of the tidal Potomac, in the Nanjemoy area of western Charles
  County, between Sandy Point (north) and Liverpool Point (south). The sanctuary sits "off the Nanjemoy Peninsula of
  Charles County, Maryland" (FR19).
- **Coordinates.** USGS GNIS feature "Mallows Bay" (ID 585669, class Bay, Widewater 7.5' quad): **38.4726° N,
  77.2686° W** (GNIS record as mirrored by HometownLocator / MyTopo). The NOAA wreck page for Yawah gives a wreck at
  38°28'28.6" N, 77°16'15.1" W (Duke 2016), which agrees.
- Access is through **Mallows Bay Park**, 1440 Wilson Landing Road, Nanjemoy, MD (county park, boat ramp and kayak
  launch). DNR bought the land in 2010 and leased it to Charles County for the park, "the main launch point for access
  to the historic shipwrecks" (FR19). The NRHP form says small-boat access was opened in summer 2011.

### How far from the White House? (measured here, 2026-11-15)

| Measure | Result | How |
|---|---|---|
| Straight line, White House (38.8977 N, 77.0365 W) to GNIS bay point | **31.9 statute miles (51.4 km)** | haversine, mean Earth radius 3,958.8 mi |
| Straight line, US Capitol to the bay | 32.0 mi | same |

What the official sources say:
- NOAA: "**40 miles south** of Washington, DC" (FR19; NOAA19 says "about 40 miles south").
- Maryland DNR: "only **30 miles downstream** from our nation's capital" (DNR).
- Shomette: "**30 miles south** of Washington, D.C." (SHOM01).

**Verdict on the title.** "30 miles from the White House" is close but slightly short: the honest straight-line figure is
**about 32 miles**. By road it is about 43 miles, and NOAA rounds to 40. Safe script wording: "about 30 miles as the
crow flies", "barely 32 miles from the White House", or "less than an hour's drive from the White House". Do not say
"30 miles by road". If a narrator needs one official number, cite Maryland DNR ("30 miles downstream") or NOAA
("40 miles south"), not both.

## B. How many wrecks? (the numbers do not agree, and that is a story point)

Every source counts something different:

| Source | Count | What is counted |
|---|---|---|
| NRHP form, Sec. 9 summary | **187** vessels | documented **in the archival record** as lost, abandoned or brought in for reduction in the Mallows Bay study area and Widewater anchorage; **177** of them Emergency Fleet |
| NRHP form, Sec. 9 | 218 | Emergency Fleet ships *scheduled* for reduction at Widewater and Mallows Bay |
| NRHP form, Sec. 9 | 154 | Emergency Fleet ships named on the Army Engineers' map of the bay, survey of 6–11 Aug 1929 (the narrative section says 152) |
| NRHP / SHOM01 / DUKE25 | **169** | ships Western Marine & Salvage had brought into Mallows Bay by 1929–31 |
| 1970 House committee report (quoted in NRHP) | 99 + 1 + 9 + 1 | hulks the Corps of Engineers identified: 99 in Mallows Bay, 1 near Sandy Point, 9 at Widewater, 1 opposite Maryland Point |
| SHOM01 (1993–95 survey) | 88 | wooden EFC ships positively identified in the bay |
| FEIS Table 1, Alternative B (the adopted boundary) | **142** "known vessels" | 104 WWI/USEFC vessels + 14 related (barges etc.) + 16 other vessels + 8 partial vessels (debris piles); plus 3 suspected and 6 non-vessel sites |
| FEIS 4.3.1 | 104 | "burnt-out remains of 104 wooden steamships" |
| NOAAWR (current web) | **141** | "known shipwrecks within the 18 square mile sanctuary"; "over 100 wooden steamships" |
| DUKE25 (drone survey, 2016 data) | **147** | wrecks in the bay covered by the orthomosaics |
| FR19 / NOAAHM23 | "more than 100" | known vessels / WWI wooden steamships |
| DNR | "nearly 200" historic shipwrecks; "over 130" wooden and composite steamships | |

**Script guidance.** Safe and defensible: "**more than 100** wooden steamships from the First World War", "**about 140
known wrecks** in all" (NOAA 141 / FEIS 142). "Nearly 200" is a Maryland DNR phrasing that matches the 187 vessels in the
archival record, not the wrecks you can see today. The title's "100+" is safe.

- **Date range.** FR19: "historic shipwrecks that date back to the Civil War, and potentially to the American
  Revolutionary War". FEIS 4.3: "from the Revolutionary War era to the present".
- **Oldest wreck.** Not settled. The NRHP form lists a buried small boat (site 18CH615) that *may* be a longboat of the
  Virginia State Navy galley *Protector*, stove in by its own crew in a skirmish with Lord Dunmore's flotilla on
  **22–23 July 1776** (NRHP Sec. 7 pp. 3, 154). It was exposed by a "freak blowout" for less than 3 hours, has copper
  nails and bronze pins, and might instead be a 1926 fishing boat. FEIS: of the 16 non-EFC vessels, "one is thought to date
  to the Civil War era and one to the last quarter of the 19th century". **Script: "possibly" a Revolutionary War boat;
  never "a 1776 wreck" as fact.**
- **Accomac** (site 18CH492): the only steel-hulled vessel in the study area (NRHP). Built **1928** at Quincy, Mass., as
  the *Virginia Lee* for the Virginia Ferry Corporation (Pennsylvania Railroad). In WWII it was requisitioned and lent to
  Britain for Halifax convoy duty, returned with freezing pipes and mechanical trouble, then hauled rubber from Brazil.
  Converted to diesel and renamed *Holiday* (Boston–Provincetown, 1949). Battered by a storm off Cape Hatteras in 1950,
  then rebuilt by the Wilson Line as the ferry *Accomac*, with a "spoon" bow, carrying 70 cars and 1,200 passengers on
  the Kiptopeke–Little Creek run. A fire on **28 May 1964** ended its service; "about 1973" it was towed into Mallows Bay
  and abandoned. An osprey nest on the starboard bow has been there since at least 1986 (NRHP; NOAAACC). It is 291.1 ft
  long, which makes it the biggest visible wreck.
  - Conflict: the FEIS says Accomac was "built in 1918" (and its Table 4 says "built in 1973"). NRHP and NOAAACC say 1928.
    **Use 1928.**
- **The "Burning Basin."** At the outlet of Mallows (Marlow's) Creek, Bethlehem Steel built two earthen cofferdams (the
  outer one reinforced with concrete and steel, with gates) plus a pump house. The method, as reconstructed from the
  remains: float hulls in, close the gate, pump the basin dry, burn the hulls down, take out the metal, re-flood (NRHP
  Sec. 7 pp. 33–34). It is now NRHP Transect 3 and is named in the sanctuary boundary (FEIS).

## C. 1917: the wooden-ship gamble

- **Why.** In February and March 1917 alone, German submarines and mines destroyed over 1,000,000 tons of merchant
  shipping, "more than 200 ships per month". More than 5,000,000 tons of Allied shipping had been sunk since 1914 (NRHP
  Sec. 7 p. 6; FEIS 4.3.1 citing Shomette). The US declared war in April 1917 (the NRHP and SHOM01 both date Wilson's
  call to arms to 2 April 1917; Congress declared war on 6 April, which is general knowledge, not checked here).
- **The idea.** In February 1917 yachtsman/engineer **F. A. (Frederic) Eustis** pitched a wooden-steamship program to
  Shipping Board chairman **William Denman**. Steel yards were busy and timber was plentiful, so cheap wooden ships could
  be built "faster than U-boats could sink them" (SHOM01). Denman publicly remarked that even if they could be sunk, "the
  United States could soon turn them out faster than the Germans could sink them" (NRHP p. 8). The press mocked the plan
  as a "bridge of wooden ships from New York to Liverpool" (Denman's complaint, NRHP p. 7).
- **Emergency Fleet Corporation (EFC)**, formed April 1917 under the Shipping Act of 1916. Capital stock: $50,000,000,
  which "eventually grew to $3,000,000,000" (NRHP p. 7). General Manager: George W. Goethals of Panama Canal fame, who
  disliked wooden ships. The **Denman–Goethals feud** ended with both resigning in July 1917 (NRHP pp. 7–8).
- **Target.** 800–1,000 wooden ships in **18 months** (NRHP p. 8; SHOM01: "1,000 wooden steamships ... within 18
  months"; DNR: "1,000 wooden merchant ships in 18 months").
- **Ferris design.** Theodore E. Ferris, official naval architect of the Shipping Board, finished plans by **July 1917**:
  281 ft 6 in overall, 46 ft beam, 3,500 long tons deadweight, 10 knots loaded, 1,400 ihp, coal-burning (NRHP pp. 9, 14;
  SHOM01: "coal burning plants"). Each needed about 1.5 million board feet of yellow pine or 1.7 million of Douglas fir
  (NRHP p. 18). Contracts were lump-sum, paid in stages at keel, framing, launch and machinery (NRHP p. 14).
- **Cost.** EFC first estimated $300,000 per hull plus $200,000 for machinery. "The actual total expense would
  eventually exceed **$750,000 per vessel**" (NRHP p. 8). SHOM01: "between $700,000 and $1 million per vessel". An AP
  dispatch of 7 Nov 1925 called the program a project "costing hundreds of millions of dollars" (PX251112). **No
  official total for the wooden program alone was found: UNVERIFIED.** Derived estimate: ~300 ships × ≥$750,000 ≈
  **$225 million+** (arithmetic only; label it "more than two hundred million dollars" at most, or avoid a total).
- **Orders.** Orders for the first **433** ships were approved in October 1917, and construction contracts for 310 were
  let a month later (NRHP p. 18; SHOM01 says the feud delayed approval of the first 433). EFC let contracts for "more than
  500" wooden steamships and later cancelled "some 200" (NRHP p. 15). A Senate probe found **731 contracted, 98
  delivered**, of which 76 had carried cargo (SHOM01; no date given). Shipyards: SHOM01 says "87 shipyards" were soon
  under contract; FR19 / FEIS / NOAAHM23 say "more than 40 shipyards in 17 states"; NOAAWR says "at least 189 shipyards
  across 26 states". **Conflict. Safe phrasing: "dozens of shipyards" (DNR wording) or "more than 40 shipyards" (the
  federal rule).**
- **Problems** (NRHP p. 18): timber big enough was hard to get (the Ferris plan called for some timbers 16 and 24 in by 40
  ft), railcars were short, labour was short, and builders were inexperienced ("an estimated eighty percent of them
  without experience", NRHP p. 8). Timber that reached the yards "was green, but was employed nevertheless". Critics
  said the ships were "badly designed, weakly constructed, poorly caulked, leaked excessively and were too small and
  expensive for long-distance cargo hauling" (SHOM01). On 1 May 1918 the Shipping Board's own Planning and Statistics
  Division doubted that wooden steamships worked for ocean service, citing small capacity and uncertain life (NRHP p. 18).
  **Range: no source gives a cruising range. "Short range" is UNVERIFIED; do not say it.**
- **Speed records.**
  - *North Bend*: the first wooden hull launched on the Pacific coast, **1 Dec 1917**, after a record 120 days of
    building, but not delivered until **24 May 1918** (NRHP p. 19). It ended up in Mallows Bay (FEIS: "the first
    steamship built by the USEFC"; NRHP calls it "the earliest USSB wooden steamer completed and certified").
  - *Aberdeen* (Grays Harbor Motorship Corp., Aberdeen, Wash.): keel laid 8:00 a.m. 9 Sep 1918, launched 9:00 p.m.
    28 Sep 1918, **17½ days** keel to launch (NRHP p. 19; FEIS gives the same dates). Its hulk is off Widewater.
  - *Coyote*: the first Ferris-type launched on the Atlantic coast, 19 Mar 1918 (Kearny, NJ), keel laid 12 Nov 1917
    (NRHP p. 18). NOAANAM says *Namecki* (Tampa) was "the first on the Atlantic Coast" completed. Different "firsts":
    launch vs completion; do not mix them.
- **The war ends first.** By October 1918 only **134** wooden steamships were complete and 263 were less than half
  finished (NRHP p. 19; SHOM01). On 11 Nov 1918 "Not a single wooden ship built during the program had sailed into a
  European port during hostilities" (NRHP p. 19). FEIS: "not a single USEFC steamship sailed into a European harbor during
  the war". NOAAWR: "none reached the theater of war". By 11 Nov, 87 wooden and 9 composite ships had been delivered,
  and 55 wooden and 7 composite had carried cargo or sailed for loading ports (NRHP p. 19). Three were lost in 1918:
  *Blackford*, *Coos Bay*, and *Dumaru*, burned by lightning near Guam (NRHP p. 19).
- **Built anyway.** Contracts kept ships coming after the Armistice: 17 delivered in Dec 1918, 37 in June 1919, the last
  deliveries by July 1920 (NRHP p. 20). **Totals:** EFC completed **296 wooden + 26 composite = 322** steamships (NRHP
  p. 21; FEIS: 322). SHOM01: "285 wooden EFC steamships built by August 1, 1920". NOAAWR: "nearly 300". Most were
  finished after the war; at least 87 + 9 = 96 had been delivered by the Armistice, so **roughly 70% were delivered
  after the war ended** (derived from NRHP figures).
- **They did work, a little.** Up to 1 Dec 1918, 101 completed, 94 in service, 305 voyages (NRHP p. 20). In
  summer–fall 1919, 167 wooden and composite ships traveled to and from Europe (NRHP p. 20); SHOM01 says 195 had made
  an Atlantic passage by Sep 1919. *Obak* made 12.01 kn on trials (NRHP p. 20). So "never sailed" is false. Correct
  line: "**not one reached Europe while the war lasted**".

## D. After the war: white elephants, the salvage company and the fires

- **Obsolete.** Oil burners replaced coal, diesels replaced steam. A wooden steamer sold for $650,000 in 1919 and
  **$100,000 in 1921** (NRHP p. 20). In 1920–21 most were moored in the **James River** off Claremont and City Point, Va.
  (NRHP p. 21). On 27 Dec 1920 the Shipping Board moved to dispose of nearly 300 wooden and composite ships
  (994,235 dwt), kept afloat by two tugs and "an army of men" at **$50,000 a month** (NRHP p. 21; SHOM01: "the grandest
  white elephant", 285 ships).
- **The sale (September 1922).** First bid: $12 per deadweight ton (rejected). Second: $430,000 for the whole fleet
  (Congress protested, rejected). Third: accepted, September 1922 (NRHP p. 21).
  - NRHP: **234 (also reported as 233)** ships, 855,931 dwt, sold for **$862,000** to George D. Perry and silent partner
    William F. Humphrey(s), who transferred them to **Western Marine & Salvage Co.** (Alexandria, Va.; HQ Portland, Ore.).
  - SHOM01: "233 ships ... sold for **$750,000**".
  - **Conflict: $750,000 vs $862,000.** The government netted "little more than **$3,300 per vessel**" (NRHP p. 22).
    Both prices round to about $3,200–3,700 per ship. **Script: "about $3,300 a ship", which is the NRHP figure and
    matches 750,000 ÷ 233.**
  - Contrast for the reversal: each cost >$750,000 to build and was sold for ~$3,300, **less than half of one percent**.
- **Plan.** 218 ships moved from the James to a War Department–approved anchorage off **Widewater, Va.**, then two at a
  time to the old Virginia Shipbuilding / Alexandria Shipbuilding plant at **Alexandria** to be stripped, then back to the
  anchorage (NRHP p. 21). Bond: $7,000 per vessel. Expected haul: 150,000–200,000 t of scrap iron/steel, 5,000 t of
  brass and copper, 162 Ferris engines, 452 boilers and more. Laid bow to stern, the fleet "would have formed a line
  **12 miles** long" (NRHP p. 22). Sec. 9 estimates 11 miles for the 218 ships and 7.76 miles for the 154 known lengths.
  Projected company profit: over $1,600,000 (NRHP p. 22).
- **Fire 1: Alexandria, Oct 1922.** *Alanthus* and *Mojave* caught fire at the wharf. A dropped cigarette in *Alanthus*'s
  engine room started it, "one of the most stubborn Alexandria fire fighters have battled". Both were saved (NRHP p. 22).
  *Alanthus* had helped rescue the crew of the submarine **S-5** in Sept 1920 (NRHP p. 22). It now lies in Mallows Bay.
- **Fire 2: Widewater, 18–19 April 1923.** A watchman knocked over a kerosene cook stove while making breakfast. The
  ships were lashed together with steel cables and only two watchmen guarded the fleet. Quantico Marines with axes
  (Capt. E. G. Huefe) and the Navy tug **USS Owl** fought it, and a Navy DH-4 seaplane filmed it. The fire burned until
  4 a.m. on 19 April. Five hulls (*Okiya, Catawba, Aberdeen, Quidnic, Gray Eagle*) burned to the waterline and sank;
  *Wasco* was scuttled; the total lost eventually reached ten. No one was hurt (NRHP pp. 22–23).
  - Conflict: the FEIS merges the two fires ("On April 18, 1923, a watchman accidentally overturned a kerosene cook stove
    ... including *Alanthus* ... 'one of the most stubborn [fires] Alexandria firefighters have battled'"). NRHP: the
    Alexandria/*Alanthus*/cigarette fire was Oct 1922; the Widewater/kerosene fire was April 1923. **Follow NRHP.**
- **Burn tests, Sept–Oct 1923.** *Aberdeen*, the record-speed ship, became the first test burn on **21 Sep 1923**: raised,
  burned, ~200 t of metal pulled, beached on Brent's Marsh, burned again (NRHP pp. 25–26). *Gray Eagle* followed.
  *Blythedale* sank while burning, and the tests were halted (NRHP p. 26). **Hook detail: the world's fastest-built ship
  was the first one they set on fire on purpose.**
- **Watermen protest.** On 29 Sep 1923, Potomac fishermen through attorney Alvin T. Embrey wrote to Commerce Secretary
  **Herbert Hoover** (copies to Interior and Navy): the anchorage flat was "the best fishing ground for gill nets for
  shad and herring on the Potomac River between its mouth and Washington", and burning debris cut their nets (NRHP
  p. 26; SHOM01).
- **Move to Maryland.** SHOM01: in **April 1924** the company bought **566 acres** around Mallows Bay. FEIS: "hundreds of
  acres". By then 123 ships lay at Widewater with ≥80 more due (SHOM01). Four marine railways, wharves and dormitories
  went up at Sandy Point (SHOM01; FEIS). The War Department permit of **24 July 1925** allowed the company "to ground,
  burn and beach in Mallow's Bay ... about two hundred hulls". It was the first official use of the name "Mallows"
  instead of "Marlow's" (NRHP p. 27).
- **The great burn, 7 November 1925.** 31 hulls were bound with steel cable and oil-soaked waste was spread on deck. At
  5 a.m., on a signal, 10 men with torches lit them. "As the torch was applied, a horde of squealing rats plunged into the
  water" (*Washington Post*, quoted in NRHP p. 27 and SHOM01). Quantico Marines, not warned, saw a glow "like the red
  ball of the sun rising in the east" and turned out to fight it (NRHP p. 27). NRHP calls it "the greatest ship
  conflagration to that date in American history"; SHOM01 "the greatest peacetime maritime coup de grace up to that time".
  - **Contemporary press (Chronicling America):** *Evening Star* 6 Nov 1925: burning of **31 wooden vessels** at
    Widewater postponed one day, under Army Engineer Maj. J. A. O'Connor (ES251106). *Sunday Star* 8 Nov 1925, p. 2:
    photo caption "burned yesterday at **Widewater, Va.**", photographed from a Marine Corps plane flown by a
    "Lieut. Schilt" out of Brown Field, Quantico, which "almost was turned over" by the heat (ES251108, OCR). AP wire
    (*Paris Express*, Ark., 12 Nov 1925): 31 hulls "taken from Widewater, Va., to **Sandy Point**, on the Maryland side",
    covered with wire netting, burned to the waterline; about **300 tons of metal per vessel** expected; **209** more
    awaiting the same fate; flames "visible for miles" (PX251112).
  - **Location conflict:** Widewater, VA (Evening Star caption; NOAA-linked historical markers) vs the Maryland side /
    Sandy Point / "in Mallows Bay" (AP; NRHP). Safe line: "**on the Potomac, off Widewater and Sandy Point**". The
    pilot "Lieut. Schilt" is probably Christian F. Schilt (at Quantico in 1925 per NHHC; Medal of Honor in Nicaragua,
    1928), but the newspaper gives only a surname, so the identity is **MED / needs a check**.
- **Slow grind, 1926–1931.** Bond releases list ships burned and hauled into the bay: 4 on 13 Jul 1926, 9 on 17 Mar 1928,
  15 on 15 Jun 1928 (including *North Bend*), 28 on 12 Oct 1928, 11 on 21 Dec 1928, 18 on 27 Apr 1929 (NRHP pp. 27–28).
  On 11 Aug 1929 the Army Engineers mapped 152 (154) named EFC hulls in the bay (NRHP). The stock market crash cut scrap
  prices (SHOM01). The company stopped work by **March–June 1931**, claiming 169 ships brought in. It sold the Sandy
  Point farm on 20 Nov 1932 and **dissolved in December 1932** (NRHP pp. 28–30). (SHOM01 and NOAAWR say "bankruptcy";
  NRHP describes abandonment and dissolution.)
  - William F. Humphrey even lobbied Army Chief of Staff **Gen. Douglas MacArthur** on 17 Jun 1931 to release the
    company's bonds (NRHP p. 29).
- **Depression scavengers, 1931–1936.** Within months "about 20 boats" were loading scrap from the burned ships, and
  "Thousands of snowy white egrets nest in these old hulks" (Sea Scout leader Fred Tilp's journal, July 1931, NRHP
  p. 29). Fifty to seventy-five Charles County residents worked the wrecks. Barge salvor Preston Dent sold scrap at **$6
  a ton** (NRHP p. 30). Dealer Harry Steinbraker bought the "metal rights" to the 169 hulls (Dec 1934, $2 a ton) and sued
  salvor Lorenzo D. Crouse. On **15 Jan 1936** Maryland's Court of Appeals (*Steinbraker v. Crouse*, 169 Md. 453) ruled the
  wrecks abandoned and **open to salvage by anyone** (NRHP pp. 31–32; FEIS 2.4).
  - Brothels and stills: SHOM01 says "At least five floating brothels and no fewer than 26 illegal stills were reportedly
    erected nearby". The NRHP form, citing the same Tilp stories, adds: "The author of this report, however, has
    found **no evidence to substantiate such claims**." The four-masted schooner *Ida S. Dow* served as the salvors'
    dormitory in 1934 and was scuttled ~1 Sep 1936. **Script: legend only ("local lore says ..."), never fact.**
  - As metal came out, the hulls got lighter and floated. On 8 Feb 1937 a hull was found 15 miles downstream in the ship
    channel, and more broke loose in April–May 1937. Engineers asked $4,600–9,500 to dike the outer ring of hulls (NRHP
    pp. 32–33).
- **Bethlehem Steel, WWII (1942–1944).** The War Production Board directive went out on 16 Jul 1942, the Metals Reserve
  Company listed Mallows Bay as a special project on 10 Oct 1942, and the project was ordered on 19 Oct 1942. Estimate:
  **up to 20,000 tons of scrap** from "over an estimated 100 ships" (NRHP pp. 33–34). SHOM01: $200,000 allocated to
  recover 20,000 t of iron from 110 hulls. Bethlehem built the cofferdam "burning basin". A dam broke in the first half of
  May 1943 (the dam repairs were later settled at $15,000). **By 31 Dec 1943 the work had cost $360,000 and yielded "a very
  small recovery of metal"** (NRHP p. 34; SHOM01). The yacht *Bodkin* (ex-USS *Nokomis*, SP-609, built 1914 for Horace
  Dodge, WWI sub-chaser off France, the only warship in the bay) was scrapped there on 22 Jun 1944. On **22 Sep 1944** the
  contract was ordered terminated. The equipment sold for $7,200 (NRHP pp. 35–36).
  - Why it failed: high costs, a dam failure, little metal left after a decade of scavenging, and a falling scrap market
    in 1944 (NRHP; FEIS: "by 1944, the demand for metal had slowed").

## E. Abandonment, nature, protection

- **Removal attempts.** In 1963 the Corps of Engineers began a **$350,000** removal effort pushed by local watermen and
  a firm called Idamont Inc. In 1968 Congress ordered the hulks destroyed under the Rivers and Harbors Act. Hearings then
  showed that Idamont was a straw company for the **Potomac Electric Power Co. (PEPCO)**, which wanted the Sandy Point
  tract for a power plant. The project was shelved (SHOM01). The NRHP form adds that PEPCO quietly promoted removal to get
  barge access for a **nuclear plant at Douglas Point**, and that on **2 Jul 1970** Maryland's DNR Secretary told Congress
  removal "would serve no public interest" (NRHP p. 36). DUKE25 gives the same outline ("Idamount Inc.").
- **1970 House committee report** (quoted in NRHP p. 36): "Seen from the air some of the hulks look like **huge
  flowerpots** ... trees have taken root in the earth inside the hulls, and these strange islands are not at all
  unattractive. Herons and egrets make their homes there. The American bald eagle nests in the area." The Interior
  Department warned that removal could stir up DDT-polluted sediment.
- **Nature takeover (survey-based):**
  - Bald eagles, herons, phoebes, red-winged blackbirds and deer on wrecks. A **beaver lodge built inside a hull**, with
    an exit hole through the hull; two beavers seen (site 18CH572, the "Concrete Wreck"; NRHP p. 154 area).
  - *Mermentau*: river otter and beaver in the hold, osprey on the mast (NRHP).
  - *Accomac*: osprey nest on the bow since at least 1986, still occupied 2013 (NRHP), and nests at bow and stern today
    (NOAAWR, NOAAACC photos).
  - *North Bend* has trees growing from the bow (NRHP, 1997 observation).
  - Mallows Bay was "the last refuge of the Potomac snowy egret and the site of Maryland's last sturgeon fishery" (SHOM01;
    NRHP p. 37). The FEIS says the Potomac area has "the second largest nesting bald eagle population in Maryland" and,
    in the management plan, "one of the largest nesting eagle populations in the eastern United States". Ospreys nest
    atop many wrecks (FEIS).
  - Sunken vessels have "trapped sediments and collected plant life becoming artificial islands" (FEIS citing Shomette).
  - Storms: Hurricane Isabel lifted *Benzonia* onto another hull (NRHP says "Isabelle in 2004"; Isabel struck in Sept
    **2003**, so the form's year is a slip). About **70% of the vessels visible in 1998** now lie just below mean low water
    (NRHP p. 37).
- **"Largest shipwreck assemblage in the Western Hemisphere": who says it, exactly.**
  - Shomette (SHOM01): "the largest shipwreck fleet in the Western Hemisphere -- and possibly the world."
  - FEIS 4.3.1: "the largest wooden ship graveyard in the Western Hemisphere (Shomette 1996)". FEIS 4.3: "one of the
    largest and most varied assemblages of submerged maritime cultural heritage resources in the Western Hemisphere".
  - NOAA web page heading (NOAAWR): "The Largest Wooden Ship Graveyard in the Western Hemisphere".
  - NRHP form (more cautious): "the largest and most densely populated ship 'graveyard' in the United States".
  - FR19: "the largest 'Ghost Fleet'" of WWI wooden steamships.
  - **Script: "what NOAA calls the largest wooden ship graveyard in the Western Hemisphere".** Do not drop "wooden"
    or claim "largest shipwreck site" unqualified.
- **Protection timeline.** 2001: Maryland GreenPrint bought the Wilson Farm tract from PEPCO ($3M state + $3M federal
    funds for several tracts) (NRHP p. 37). 2010: DNR land leased to Charles County for Mallows Bay Park (FR19). 16 Sep
    2014: Maryland's nomination for an 18 sq mi area, backed by 60+ organizations (DNR). 12 Jan 2015: NOAA added it to
    the inventory of nominations (FR19). **24 Apr 2015: NRHP listing**, ref. 15000173 (FR19). 9 Jan 2017: proposed rule
    for ~52 sq mi (FR19). 8 Jul 2019: final rule for 18 sq mi (FR19). **3 Sep 2019: designation effective** (84 FR
    50736; NOAAABT; DNR).
- **Size.** **18 square miles** (13.6 sq nmi, ~47 km²) (FR19; FEIS). The NRHP district itself is ~17 sq mi (FEIS). The
    "52 sq km?" in the brief is wrong: **52 square miles** was the larger Alternative C proposed in 2017, which was not
    adopted (FR19). 18 sq mi = 46.6 km² (conversion).
- **Firsts.** "the first national marine sanctuary in nearly 20 years" (NOAAABT); "the first new national marine
    sanctuary in two decades"; the previous youngest was Thunder Bay (2000) (NOAA19). Also Maryland's first national
    marine sanctuary and the Chesapeake Bay's first (DNR; NOAA19). It is jointly managed by NOAA, Maryland and Charles
    County.
- **Today (2024–2026).** Kayak access from Mallows Bay Park; guided tours run in season. 2024 Maryland GovPics photos
    show a kayak tour (Commons). Duke University drone orthomosaics of all 147 wrecks were published in *Scientific
    Data* in Sept 2025 (DUKE25). NOAA's 2023 needs assessment lists monitoring of wreck movement, fires, storms and
    vandalism as priorities (NOAAHM23). NOAA was running "The Final Salvage" talk on 9 Nov 2026 (NOAA home page, visited
    2026-11-15). No visitor-number statistics were found (FR19 says none existed in 2019): **UNVERIFIED**.

## F. Story beats: the "answer", the reversal, and the surprises (all verified unless noted)

**Candidate answer for the last act:** *The ships that were built to win a war, never reached it, and were burned
as junk became the most protected wrecks on the river. A court declared them abandoned in 1936, Congress ordered
them destroyed in 1968, and in 2019 the federal government made them a national marine sanctuary.* Junk to national
treasure. The salvagers lost money (Western Marine dissolved; Bethlehem spent $360k for "a very small recovery"), yet
the hulls became habitat: eagles, ospreys, beavers, trees.

**Mid-video reversal options:**
1. The 1960s plan to clear the bay "for the watermen" was secretly a power company's land grab for a nuclear plant site
   (SHOM01; NRHP). Removing the wrecks, not the wrecks themselves, turned out to be the threat.
2. Built in a frenzy to beat the U-boats, ~70% were delivered after the war ended (derived from NRHP).
3. The world's fastest-built ship (*Aberdeen*, 17½ days) became the first test burn (NRHP).

**Top surprising, verified facts:**
1. Each ship cost over $750,000 to build and was sold for about $3,300: less than 0.5% (NRHP).
2. Not one wooden steamship reached a European port while the war lasted (NRHP; FEIS; NOAAWR).
3. 31 ships were burned in one blaze on 7 Nov 1925. Rats fled the decks, and Marines at Quantico thought it was a
   disaster (NRHP; SHOM01; Evening Star; AP).
4. The government paid Bethlehem Steel's wartime salvage bill, and $360,000 bought "a very small recovery of metal"
   (NRHP).
5. A 1936 court ruling made the wrecks free for anyone to strip (NRHP; FEIS).
6. Beavers built a lodge inside one hull, and ospreys have nested on the ferry *Accomac* since at least 1986 (NRHP).
7. The plan to destroy the wrecks in the 1960s fronted for a utility (PEPCO) and was shelved after hearings (SHOM01;
   NRHP).
8. The bay holds the first EFC steamship completed (*North Bend*) and some of the last (*Boyton, Munra, Wonahbe,
   Owatam*) (FEIS).
9. Laid bow to stern, the fleet would have stretched about 11–12 miles (NRHP).

**Avoid (unverified or false):** "short range"; "never sailed"; total program cost to the dollar; "1776 wreck" as fact;
brothels and stills as fact; "52 sq km"; "30 miles by road".
