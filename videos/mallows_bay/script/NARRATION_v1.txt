Right now, about thirty miles from the White House, more than a hundred ships are rotting in the Potomac River.

Not one or two. A whole fleet. The United States built them to win the First World War.

Each one cost the government more than seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars. Most of them were finished after the war was already over.

Then the government sold them for about three thousand dollars each. A salvage company set them on fire. Thirty-one of them burned in a single day.

And still, they would not go away. A steel giant tried to break them up. Congress ordered them removed. For a hundred years, everyone who tried to get rid of this fleet failed.

So why are they still here? The answer is hiding in the wrecks themselves. And it is not what anyone in Washington planned.

Start with what you would see from the air. On the Maryland shore of the Potomac, in Charles County, near a place called Nanjemoy, there is a small bay. Mallows Bay.

At low tide, dark shapes rise out of the water. Long ovals, all about the same size, lying side by side. Some are bare ribs. Some carry whole trees.

Each shape is the hull of a wooden steamship. Federal surveys count more than a hundred of them from the First World War alone. NOAA counts one hundred and forty-one known shipwrecks in and around the bay.

The bay is thirty-two miles from the White House, as the crow flies. Yet most people in Washington have never heard of it.

To understand how a fleet ended up here, go back to the spring of nineteen seventeen.

In February and March of nineteen seventeen, German U-boats sank more than a million tons of shipping. Britain was running out of ships faster than it could build them.

On the sixth of April, the United States declared war on Germany. Now it had to send an army across the Atlantic. And it did not have the ships to carry it.

Ten days later, the government created the Emergency Fleet Corporation. Its job was to build ships faster than the U-boats could sink them.

Steel was short. Shipyards were full. So someone proposed something that sounded almost old-fashioned. Build the ships out of wood.

America had forests, and it had carpenters. The plan called for as many as a thousand wooden steamships in eighteen months.

The standard design came from a naval architect named Theodore Ferris. A wooden hull about two hundred and eighty-one feet long. It carried about three thousand five hundred tons of cargo, and it burned coal.

Its top speed was about ten knots. Slow, even then. But it could be built almost anywhere there was timber and water.

By nineteen nineteen, the government counted eighty-seven yards building wooden ships. On the Pacific coast, on the Gulf coast and along the Atlantic.

At the peak, in September nineteen eighteen, more than eighty thousand people were building wooden ships.

The first one slid into the water in Oregon on the first of December, nineteen seventeen. She was called the North Bend. Remember that name.

In Aberdeen, Washington, a yard claimed a record. A hull built from keel to launch in seventeen and a half days. They named her the Aberdeen.

On paper, it looked like a miracle. In the water, it was something else.

The timber was often green. Freshly cut wood shrinks as it dries, and the seams of the hulls opened. Many of the ships leaked.

Wood is not as strong as steel, so a wooden hull could only be built so long. The ships were small for ocean cargo, and they burned a lot of coal. The government's own shipping board said they were suited only for short trips.

And they were late. From August nineteen eighteen, the yards were finally delivering about one wooden ship a day. But by the end of October, only ninety-two wooden and composite ships had been delivered.

Then, on the eleventh of November nineteen eighteen, the war ended.

According to the government's own historic survey, not one of the wooden ships reached a port in Europe while the fighting lasted.

But the yards kept building. Contracts had been signed. Workers had to be paid. Hundreds of hulls were already on the ways.

In the end, three hundred and twenty-two of them were finished. About seven in ten were delivered after the war was already over.

By the middle of nineteen twenty, the shipping board had spent more than two hundred and seventy-four million dollars on wooden and composite ships.

For a short time after the war, they were put to work. In April nineteen twenty, as many as two hundred and forty of them were in service. Some of them did cross the Atlantic, carrying coal and cargo.

Then world shipping slumped. There were suddenly too many ships, and nobody wanted slow, leaky wooden ones.

So the government tried to sell them. In nineteen nineteen and nineteen twenty, the board was getting about two hundred thousand dollars a ship.

By nineteen twenty-one, it sold twenty-one of them for about five thousand seven hundred dollars each.

The rest were tied up in long rows, waiting. The biggest gathering was in the James River, in Virginia.

On the twelfth of September nineteen twenty-two, the government gave up. It auctioned off two hundred and thirty-three wooden steamships in a single lot.

The price was seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars. For all of them. That is about three thousand two hundred dollars a ship.

The buyer's company was called Western Marine and Salvage. And the deal came with one condition. The ships could never sail as steamers again. They had to be broken up.

The plan was simple. Strip out the engines, the boilers and everything metal. Then burn the wooden hulls down to the waterline and collect what was left.

The company towed the ships up the Potomac. Some went to Alexandria, just across the river from Washington. Most were anchored off Widewater, Virginia, near the Marine base at Quantico.

It went wrong almost at once. In October nineteen twenty-two, a fire broke out on two of the ships at Alexandria, blamed on a cigarette.

In April nineteen twenty-three, fire broke out again, this time at Widewater. Five ships were destroyed. One of them was the Aberdeen, the record-breaker. She was raised, and later burned again, as a test.

The local watermen were furious. Their shad and herring nets were in the same water. Sunken hulls and floating wreckage threatened their living. They complained all the way to Washington.

So the company looked for a place out of the way. In April nineteen twenty-four, it bought land on the Maryland side, across from Widewater. Around a quiet bay.

Mallows Bay.

On the seventh of November nineteen twenty-five, the company tried to finish the job in one go.

At five in the morning, thirty-one ships were set on fire at once.

Across the river, at the Marine base at Quantico, sentries saw the glow. The newspaper said it looked like the red ball of the sun. A Marine Corps plane flew over and photographed the blaze. Above the flames, the paper said, the plane was nearly flipped over.

One reporter wrote that as the decks burned, rats poured off the ships.

When it was over, the company had what it came for. A little iron and steel, pulled from the ashes.

After that, the company gave up on burning ships in big batches. It burned them one at a time.

Over the next years, the burned hulls were packed into Mallows Bay. In all, the company said, it brought one hundred and sixty-nine ships here.

It never made the money it hoped for. Then came the Great Depression. In nineteen thirty-one, the company stopped work. By the end of nineteen thirty-two, it had been dissolved.

The ships were still there.

Now local people moved in. In the hungry years of the Depression, families rowed out to the wrecks and pried out bolts, spikes and iron, and sold them as scrap.

The fight over the scrap ended up in court. In January nineteen thirty-six, Maryland's highest court ruled that the wrecks had been abandoned. Anyone could take what was left.

That April, an Army camera plane flew over the bay. It caught the fleet lying in rows, hull against hull.

There is an old story that the wrecks hid floating brothels and whiskey stills in those years. It is a good story. But the historian who wrote the official survey found no evidence for it.

Then, in nineteen forty-one, America went to war again. And this time, the wrecks seemed to have one more job to do.

The new war needed steel. Every scrap drive in the country was collecting pots, pans and old cars. And in Mallows Bay, there were thousands of tons of iron sitting in the mud.

In nineteen forty-two, the government hired one of the biggest steel companies in the country. Bethlehem Steel.

The plan was clever. Bethlehem built a basin at the edge of the bay, with dams and gates. Hulls would be floated in, the water pumped out, and the wood burned away on dry ground.

The target was up to twenty thousand tons of scrap metal.

It did not work. According to the records, by the end of nineteen forty-three Bethlehem had spent three hundred and sixty thousand dollars. And the result was, quote, a very small recovery of metal.

In September nineteen forty-four, the government ended the contract. The fleet that missed one world war had now missed a second.

The basin is still there. You can still see its outline from the air, at the mouth of a creek at the edge of the bay.

After the war, the wrecks were mostly forgotten. Then, in October nineteen sixty-three, a company called Idamont bought the farm on Sandy Point, right beside the bay.

The Army Corps of Engineers offered a cheap fix. Pin the outer wrecks in place with stone, for about fifty thousand dollars. The landowner said no. It wanted the wrecks gone.

In nineteen sixty-eight, Congress ordered the wrecks removed, at an estimated three hundred and fifty thousand dollars, if Maryland paid half. Maryland would not pay.

And here is the twist. In nineteen seventy, at a hearing in Congress, a vice-president of the power company PEPCO was asked about Idamont. Under oath, he called it a dummy corporation, formed by PEPCO.

The power company had been quietly buying the land beside the fleet for a new power plant. It denied any part in the push to remove the wrecks. But the wrecks were right in front of its land.

That same year, the Army counted ninety-nine hulks still in Mallows Bay. And Maryland's natural resources department said clearing them would serve no public interest.

Because while everyone had been arguing about the ships, something else had moved in.

To answer the question, go back out on the water.

When the hulls were burned, their bottoms were left in the mud, packed with dirt and ash. Over the years, river silt filled them up.

Seeds blew in. Then bushes. Then trees. By nineteen seventy, whole trees were growing out of the hulks, like plants in giant flowerpots.

The wrecks had become islands. Ship-shaped islands, with their own trees.

Fish moved into the ribs. Largemouth bass, striped bass, shad, herring.

Ospreys built nests on the high timbers. Bald eagles hunt over the bay.

On one wreck, surveyors found a beaver lodge built inside the hull. The beavers had chewed an exit hole right through the side of the ship.

Not every wreck here is from the First World War. At the edge of the bay lies a steel car ferry, the Accomac. She was built in nineteen twenty-eight, burned in nineteen sixty-four, and was towed here years later.

Ospreys have been nesting on her since at least nineteen eighty-six.

And somewhere in the bay lies the North Bend. The very first wooden ship of the program. The ship that started it all ended up here too.

In April twenty fifteen, the wrecks were listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

And in September twenty nineteen, the bay and the river around it became the Mallows Bay-Potomac River National Marine Sanctuary. Eighteen square miles. The first new national marine sanctuary in nearly twenty years.

NOAA calls it the largest wooden ship graveyard in the Western Hemisphere. Today you can paddle a kayak through the fleet, right over the ribs of ships built to fight the Kaiser.

So that is the answer. The fleet is still here because every attempt to get rid of it failed. And while those attempts failed, the river turned the fleet into something nobody planned. A habitat.

The same government that paid a fortune to build these ships, and then sold them off to be destroyed, now protects them by law.

But the fleet will not last forever. About seven in ten of the wrecks that could be seen in nineteen ninety-eight now sit just under the surface, even at low tide.

So here is the question. Should we try to save what is left of it? Or let the river finish the job? Tell us what you think in the comments.
